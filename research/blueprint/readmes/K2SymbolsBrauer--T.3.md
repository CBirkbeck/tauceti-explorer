## T.3:localization-comparison — Residues, transfers and the localisation boundary

Finite support is the whole of one lemma and is genuinely short: the symbol is trivial wherever
both valuations vanish, and the pinned finiteness of the set of places where an element has
nonzero order does the rest. The higher Milnor residues and specialisation maps are Serre's
construction, with the relation on the indeterminate stated rather than assumed, since without
it the map does not kill the Steinberg elements. The transfer and the norm-residue formula
follow, and the identification with the boundary of the localisation sequence is a
**comparison**: the sequence belongs to `SchemeKTheoryOperations:S.3` and `EllipticKTheory:E.3`,
and the sign is fixed at the comparison rather than by adjusting the symbol.

### `finite-support` — Finite support of the residues

*lemma*

For a field with a family of discrete valuations in which every element has nonzero valuation at
only finitely many members, the tame symbol of a pair of nonzero elements is trivial at all but
finitely many valuations, and the same holds for a finite sum of symbols. The proof is the
observation that the symbol is trivial whenever both valuations vanish.

**Hypotheses.** The family is the set of places of a function field, or the height-one primes of a Dedekind domain.

**Construction and proof.**

1. Import the finiteness of the set of places at which a given element has nonzero order, in
   either of its two pinned forms.
1. Observe that the tame symbol of a pair is trivial at every valuation where both arguments
   have valuation zero.
1. Intersect the two finite exceptional sets to bound the support of a single symbol, and take a
   finite union for a finite sum.
1. Record that this is the entire finiteness argument: no deeper input is needed.

**Acceptance.**

- The support of the symbol of a pair is contained in the union of the two supports.
- For a finite sum of symbols the support is the union of the supports of the summands.
- For two units of the ring of integers the symbol is trivial everywhere, so the support is
  empty.

**Depends on.** **inside this roadmap** `tame-symbol`; **baseline** `tauceti:TauCeti.Place.finite_setOf_ord_ne_zero`, `mathlib:FractionalIdeal.finite_factors`.

**Source.** Kbook.2013, III.6.3 (PDF p. 242): “The units R^x form the set v^{-1}(0), and the maximal ideal of R is generated by any pi in R with v(pi) = 1.” — The valuation-zero condition under which the symbol is trivial, which is the finiteness argument.

### `higher-milnor-residues` — Higher Milnor residues and specialisation, with their product signs ★

*construction* · planet **Higher Milnor residues**

For a discrete valuation on F there are two surjections out of the Milnor K-group in degree n:
the higher residue to the degree n minus one group of the residue field, and the specialisation
map to the degree n group of the residue field, attached to a chosen uniformiser. They are
determined by their values on symbols of units times powers of the uniformiser, and the product
signs are those of the anticommutative algebra in which they are constructed. In degree two the
residue is the tame symbol of T.3 and the specialisation is the leading-coefficient map.

**Hypotheses.** v is a discrete valuation on F with residue field k and a chosen uniformiser.

**Construction and proof.**

1. Form the graded algebra over the Milnor K-theory of the residue field generated by an
   indeterminate in degree one, subject to the relation that its square is the symbol of minus
   one with it. That relation is what makes the construction work, and it is stated rather than
   assumed.
1. Define a homomorphism from the units of F to the degree-one part sending a unit times a power
   of the uniformiser to the class of the reduced unit plus that power times the indeterminate.
1. Prove that it kills the Steinberg elements, by the three cases the source performs: both
   arguments units, one argument of positive valuation, and one argument outside the valuation
   ring.
1. Extend to a graded ring map by the presentation of Milnor K-theory, and split the target
   degreewise into the two summands to obtain the two maps.
1. Prove the displayed formulas on symbols and the product signs, and identify the degree-two
   cases with the symbols of T.3, recording the inversion between the two normalisations.
1. Prove surjectivity of both maps, and the dependence of the specialisation on the uniformiser.

### `rigidity` — Rigidity for a complete discretely valued field

*theorem*

If F is complete for the valuation, with residue field k, then for every integer q prime to the
characteristic of k the pair consisting of the specialisation map and the residue is an
isomorphism from the Milnor K-group modulo q onto the direct sum of the corresponding groups of
the residue field, in every degree.

**Hypotheses.** F is complete for v; q is prime to the residue characteristic.

**Construction and proof.**

1. State the map as the pair of the two maps of the previous node, reduced modulo q.
1. Prove the isomorphism in the stated range, following the source.
1. Record the hypothesis: without primality to the residue characteristic the statement fails,
   so the wild case is excluded.

**Acceptance.**

- The statement is modulo q, not integrally.
- The hypothesis on q is necessary.
- In degree one the statement is the decomposition of the unit group of a complete discretely
  valued field modulo q.

**Depends on.** **inside this roadmap** `higher-milnor-residues`.

**Source.** Kbook.2013, III.7.3.1 (PDF p. 254): “Corollary 7.3.1 (Rigidity). Suppose that F is complete with respect to the valuation v, with residue field k = k_v. For every integer q prime to char(k), the maps lambda plus partial_v : K^M_n(F)/q -> K^M_n(k)/q + K^M_{n-1}(k)/q are isomorphisms for all n.” — The corollary, as displayed.

## T.3:symbols — The tame symbol

The tame symbol, built through **unit parts** so that the residue map is never applied to a
nonintegral element — the pinned decomposition of a nonzero element as a unit times a power of
the uniformiser is exactly what makes that possible. One thing is stated and not smoothed over:
the roadmap's normalisation `∂_v{f,g} = (−1)^{v(f)v(g)} · overline(f^{v(g)}/g^{v(f)})` is the
**inverse** of the K-book's, so `∂_v{u,π} = ū` here and `∂_v{π,u} = ū` there. The definition
records the inversion and a unit test catches a silent reversal. Bilinearity and the Steinberg
relation come from the source's four-case analysis, and the ramification formula `∂_w = ∂_v^e`
closes the layer.

### `tame-symbol` — The tame symbol of a discrete valuation ★

*definition* · planet **Tame symbol**

For a discrete valuation v on a field F with residue field k define the tame symbol of a pair of
nonzero elements by the sign minus one to the power v(f) times v(g), times the residue of f to
the power v(g) divided by g to the power v(f). The quotient inside the residue has valuation
zero, and the construction is implemented through unit parts, so the residue map is never
applied to a nonintegral element. In this normalisation the symbol of a unit with a uniformiser
is the residue of the unit, and the symbol of a uniformiser with a unit is its inverse.

**Hypotheses.** v is a discrete valuation on the field F, with valuation ring R, residue field k and a uniformiser. f and g are nonzero elements of F.

**Construction and proof.**

1. Decompose each argument as a unit times a power of the uniformiser, using the pinned
   decomposition for a surjective discrete valuation.
1. Form the displayed combination in terms of the two unit parts and the two valuations, so that
   the expression whose residue is taken is visibly a unit of the valuation ring.
1. Apply the pinned residue map from the units of the valuation subring to the units of the
   residue field.
1. Prove that the result does not depend on the chosen decomposition, and record the
   normalisation on the two mixed pairs.
1. Record the convention explicitly against the source: the K-book's tame symbol is the inverse
   of this one, since it takes the residue of g to the power v(f) over f to the power v(g). The
   roadmap fixes this normalisation, and every comparison below states the inversion rather than
   silently reversing a formula.

### `tame-symbol-steinberg` — The tame symbol is bilinear and satisfies the Steinberg relation ★

*theorem* · planet **The tame symbol is a Steinberg symbol**

The tame symbol is bilinear in each argument and satisfies the Steinberg relation: it is trivial
whenever the two arguments sum to one. It therefore descends to a surjective homomorphism from
K_2(F) to the units of the residue field.

**Hypotheses.** v is a discrete valuation on F; the arguments are nonzero.

**Construction and proof.**

1. Bilinearity is immediate from the unit-part expression, since both the sign and the residue
   are multiplicative in each exponent.
1. For the Steinberg relation run the four cases of the source. If the first valuation is
   positive the first argument lies in the maximal ideal, so the second is a unit and the symbol
   is the residue of the second to the power of the first valuation, which is one. The case of a
   positive second valuation is symmetric, and the case of two zero valuations is trivial.
1. If the first valuation is negative then the inverse of the first argument has positive
   valuation, and the quotient of one minus the argument by the argument is congruent to minus
   one; since the two valuations agree the sign and the residue cancel.
1. Apply Matsumoto's presentation, imported from the companion part, to descend the symbol to a
   homomorphism out of K_2(F).
1. Prove surjectivity from the normalisation on a mixed pair.

**Acceptance.**

- The four cases of the Steinberg computation are exhaustive and are all written out.
- The descended map is onto the units of the residue field.
- The Steinberg relation holds in the roadmap's normalisation as well as in the source's, since
  the two differ by an inversion.

**Depends on.** **inside this roadmap** `tame-symbol`, `tame-symbol-uniformizer-independence`, `K2SymbolsBrauer:T.2`.

**Source.** Kbook.2013, III.6.3 proof (PDF p. 242): “To see that partial_v(r, s) = 1 when r + s = 1 we consider several cases. If v_1 > 0 then r is in the maximal ideal, so s = 1 - r is a unit and partial_v(r, s) is the residue of s^{v_1}, which is 1.” — The case analysis, as displayed.

### `ramification-formula` — Behaviour under a finite extension: the ramification formula

*lemma*

Let E be a finite extension of F and w a valuation of E over v, with ramification index e. Then
for nonzero elements of F the tame symbol at w of the pair is the e-th power of the tame symbol
at v, read in the units of the larger residue field. If the extension is unramified at every
prime above v, the diagonal inclusion of the residue units is compatible with the product of the
symbols above.

**Hypotheses.** E is a finite extension of F, w lies over v, and e is the ramification index.

**Construction and proof.**

1. Use that the valuation w restricted to F is e times v, and substitute into the unit-part
   expression.
1. Deduce the displayed power formula, checking that the sign is also raised to the e-th power
   correctly, which is where the parity of e enters.
1. For the unramified case, apply the formula at each prime above v and compose with the
   diagonal inclusion.

**Acceptance.**

- For an unramified extension the formula is the identity on residues followed by the inclusion.
- The parity of the ramification index matters for the sign, and the statement records it.
- The formula is about elements of the smaller field; for elements of the larger field there is
  no such formula, and the transfer is the right tool.

**Depends on.** **inside this roadmap** `tame-symbol-steinberg`; **baseline** `mathlib:Ideal.sum_ramification_inertia_eq_finrank`.

**Source.** Kbook.2013, III.6.3.1 (PDF p. 242): “Remark 6.3.1 (Ramification). ... Then there is an integer e, called the ramification index, such that w(r) = e v(r) for every r in F. The natural map K_2(F) -> K_2(E) is compatible with the tame symbols in the sense that for every r_1, r_2 in F^x we have partial_w(r_1, r_2) = partial_v(r_1, r_2)^e in k_w^x.” — The formula and the unramified refinement, as displayed.

## T.4 — Rational function fields and reciprocity

The Bass–Tate sequence, with the place at infinity deliberately **outside** the sum — the
polynomial primes alone do not give reciprocity, and the node says so. The transfers come from
that sequence, and transitivity, the substantial part, is independence of the chosen chain of
simple extensions. Weil reciprocity then reduces to the projective line by the transfer and
falls out of the sequence together with the residue at infinity; in the disjoint-support case it
is the classical symmetric evaluation that Tau Ceti's divisor apparatus already expresses, which
is the acceptance test.

### `bass-tate-sequence` — The Bass-Tate sequence for a rational function field ★

*theorem* · planet **Bass-Tate sequence**

For a field F and the rational function field in one variable there is an exact sequence whose
first map is the inclusion of the Milnor K-theory of F and whose second is the sum of the
residues at the closed points of the affine line, in every degree. The place at infinity is not
one of those closed points, and the sequence together with the residue at infinity is what
yields the reciprocity formula for the projective line; the polynomial primes alone do not.

**Hypotheses.** F is a field; the function field is the rational one in one variable.

**Construction and proof.**

1. Import the classification of the places of a rational function field as the height-one primes
   of the polynomial ring together with the place at infinity.
1. Construct the sum of the residues at the finite places, using the higher residues of T.3, and
   prove that it is well defined by the finite-support lemma.
1. Prove exactness at the two spots, following the source's filtration of Milnor K-theory of the
   function field by the degrees of the polynomials involved.
1. Prove that the sequence splits, and record the splitting the specialisation maps give.
1. State the role of the place at infinity: the residue there is determined by the finite
   residues through the reciprocity relation of the next node, and the sequence as stated omits
   it.

**Acceptance.**

- In degree one the sequence is the divisor sequence of the affine line.
- The place at infinity is omitted from the sum, which is why the sequence alone does not give
  reciprocity.
- Exactness fails if the residues are taken over all places including infinity, which is a non-
  example the statement records.

**Depends on.** **inside this roadmap** `higher-milnor-residues`, `finite-support`, `K2SymbolsBrauer:T.2`; **baseline** `tauceti:TauCeti.Place.ratFuncEquiv`, `tauceti:TauCeti.Place.heightOneSpectrumEquiv`.

**Source.** Kbook.2013, III.6.4.1 and III.7.3 (PDF pp. 243, 254): “Proposition 6.4.1. (Bass-Tate) ... the higher tame symbols partial_v : K^M_n(F) -> K^M_{n-1}(k_v)” — The residues out of which the sequence is assembled; the sequence is attributed to Bass and Tate, whose paper was not obtained.

### `milnor-transfer-transitivity` — Milnor transfers from the Bass-Tate sequence, and their transitivity

*theorem*

### `k2-of-a-finite-field` — K_2 of a finite field is trivial

*theorem*

For every finite field the second K-group is trivial. The proof is elementary and is given in
full by the source: let x generate the cyclic multiplicative group; by Matsumoto's presentation
it is enough to show that the symbol of x with itself vanishes. If the field has even order
then the symbol of x with itself equals the symbol of x with its negative, which vanishes.
Otherwise the symbol of x with itself squares to one by skew-symmetry, so it equals the symbol
of any odd power of x with any odd power of x; the odd powers of x are exactly the non-squares,
so it is enough to find a non-square u with 1 - u also a non-square, and such a u exists
because u maps to 1 - u is an involution of the set of elements different from 0 and 1, a set
with (q-1)/2 non-squares and only (q-3)/2 squares.

**Hypotheses.**

- F is a finite field with q elements; q is a prime power.
- Matsumoto's presentation of the second K-group of a field by Steinberg symbols is imported
  from the companion part of this roadmap, T.2.
- Skew-symmetry of the symbol, that the symbol of x with itself is the symbol of x with minus
  one, is also imported from T.2.

**Construction and proof.**

1. Reduce to the generator by Matsumoto's presentation: the group is generated by the symbols,
   which are bilinear, so the symbol of a generator with itself generates.
1. Dispose of even q: the symbol of x with itself is the symbol of x with its negative, which
   is trivial since minus one is one.
1. For odd q, use skew-symmetry to see that the symbol of x with itself has order dividing two,
   hence equals the symbol of any odd power of x with any odd power of x.
1. Identify the odd powers of x with the non-squares.
1. Count: the involution sending u to 1 - u acts on the set of elements different from 0 and 1,
   which has (q-1)/2 non-squares and (q-3)/2 squares, so some non-square u has 1 - u a non-
   square.
1. Conclude that the symbol of x with itself is a Steinberg symbol of two non-squares and hence
   trivial, so the group vanishes.

**Acceptance.**

- The statement is for every finite field, including characteristic two, and the proof splits
  into those two cases.
- The counting step is what makes the argument work and is where the finiteness enters; the
  same statement is false for a general field.
- The result is what makes the tame symbol at a finite place land in a group with no K_2, which
  is the shape of the localisation sequence of this layer.

**Depends on.** **inside this roadmap** `unramified-subgroup`; **other roadmaps** `K2SymbolsBrauer:T.2`, `KTheoryFiniteLocalFields:L.1`.

**Source.** Kbook.2013, III.6.1.1 (PDF p. 231): “Corollary 6.1.1. K_2(F_q) = 1 for every finite
field F_q. Proof. If x generates the cyclic group F_q^times, we must show that the generator x
tensor x of the cyclic group F_q^times tensor F_q^times vanishes in K_2. If q is even, then
{x,x} = {x,-x} = 1, so we may suppose that q is odd. Since {x,x}^2 = 1 by skew-symmetry, we
have {x,x} = {x,x}^{mn} = {x^m, x^n} for every odd m and n.” — The statement and the first half
of the proof, verbatim.

**Source.** Kbook.2013, III.6.1.1, the counting step (PDF p. 231): “Since odd powers of x are
the same as non-squares, it suffices to find a non-square u such that 1 - u is also a non-
square. But such a u exists because u maps to (1-u) is an involution on the set F_q minus
{0,1}, and this set consists of (q-1)/2 non-squares but only (q-3)/2 squares.” — The counting

### `certified-presentation` — What a certified finite presentation is, and the rule about upper bounds

*definition*

A certified finite presentation of a tame kernel is a finite list of generators, a finite list
of relations among them verified in the group, a proof that the listed generators generate, and
a proof that the relations generate all relations. The first three give an UPPER bound on the
group: a surjection from the finitely presented group onto it. The fourth is what turns the
bound into an isomorphism, and it is the part that is usually missing. The definition records
the rule the roadmap insists on: an upper bound together with a surjective presentation is not
an isomorphism, and may not be reported as one without a matching lower bound or a complete
kernel argument. A lower bound is any construction of enough distinct elements or of a
surjection onto a known group, the sign symbol and the tame symbols being the two available
here.

**Hypotheses.**

- The group is the tame kernel of a number field, or the second K-group of its ring of
  S-integers.
- Generation and the completeness of the relations are two separate obligations; the
  certificate carries both as data with their proofs.
- The instances belong to ArithmeticKTheory N.6; what this layer owns is the format and the
  rule.

**Construction and proof.**

1. Define the certificate as a record: generators, relations, a generation proof, and a
   completeness proof for the relations.
1. Define the upper bound attached to the first three: the surjection from the finitely
   presented group.
1. Define a lower bound as a surjection onto a group of known order, or an explicit family of
   distinct elements, and record the two sources of lower bounds available in this layer: the
   sign symbol at a real place and the tame symbols at the finite places.
1. State the soundness rule: the order of the group is determined only when an upper and a
   lower bound agree, or when the kernel of the presentation is computed.
1. State the one-line consequence for the layer: no node may report an order or an isomorphism
   from a surjective presentation alone.

### `dennis-stein-symbol` — Dennis-Stein symbols ★

*definition* · planet **Dennis-Stein symbols**

For commuting elements of a ring whose product differs from one by a unit, define the Dennis-
Stein symbol as the displayed word in the Steinberg group; it lies in K_2 because its image in
the elementary group is trivial. It is independent of the two indices, and when the first entry
is a unit it equals the Steinberg symbol of that entry with one minus the product. The symbols
satisfy three relations, and the modern sign convention is used, which differs from the pre-1980
literature.

**Hypotheses.** R is an associative unital ring; the two elements commute and one minus their product is a unit.

**Construction and proof.**

1. Write the displayed word and check that its image in the elementary group is trivial, so that
   it lies in K_2.
1. Prove independence of the two indices.
1. Prove that for a unit first entry it reduces to a Steinberg symbol.
1. Prove the three relations: the symmetry relation, the addition relation, and the product
   relation.
1. Record the sign convention and the consequence that the symbol with a one entry is trivial.
1. Record the relative form: when one entry lies in an ideal the symbol is an element of the
