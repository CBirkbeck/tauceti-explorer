# Integral A_inf cohomology: coefficients, décalage and the proper construction

## Scope and shared objects

This part concerns AI.0–AI.5, including the two coefficient substages. It uses cohomological grading, with a differential of degree +1. An invertible ideal is a locally free rank-one ideal subsheaf, not an arbitrary principal ideal generated by a zero divisor. Negative powers of an invertible ideal are powers of its dual. Completion of a complex means derived completion unless explicitly called ordinary completion.

The main geometric input is a smooth proper formal scheme over O_C, where C is a complete algebraically closed nonarchimedean extension of Q_p. Neither proper finiteness nor finite-free cohomology is an assertion about an arbitrary smooth affine. The construction of Lη is independent of these geometric hypotheses and must remain available to CrystallineCohomology CR.4 before the construction or crystalline comparison of AΩ.

There is one set of coefficient rings, module categories and derived categories. PerfectoidQuotients Q0:integral-algebra supplies the integral perfectoid prefix; CrystallineCohomology CR.0 supplies PD envelopes; PadicHodgeTheory R06.1 supplies rational period rings. EnhancedDerivedSheaves E1 supplies module sheaves, resolutions, derived tensor and the comparison with the chosen ordinary derived category. DerivedDeRhamCohomology DD.1 owns generic derived completion. EnhancedDerivedSheaves E2 supplies repleteness and inverse-limit infrastructure, while E4 applies DD.1 to sheaves. None is reconstructed by giving a new structure fields that assume its theorems.

The accepted RS-01 division is retained: AI.0:period-comparison owns the integral–rational coefficient comparison, and AI.5 owns the generic BMS1 §4.2 torsion-specialization package. CohomologyComparisons CP.0 and CP.5 import these and retain their geometric diagrams and applications.

## AI.1. The décalage operator

### 1. Principal divisibility and the divided differential

Let R be commutative, f∈R, and C an integer-indexed cochain complex of R-modules. Define

\[
 E_f(C)^i=\{x\in C^i:\text{there exists }y\in C^{i+1}\text{ with }fy=d x\}.
\]

This is the preimage under d of the image of multiplication by f. Adding witnesses and multiplying a witness by a scalar proves that it is a submodule; its definition does not require f-injectivity. In particular, E_0(C)^i is the kernel of d, whereas E_1(C)^i is the entire term.

Assume now that multiplication by f is injective on every term of C. The witness y is unique. Define δ_i(x)=y. Since f·d y=d²x=0, injectivity on C^(i+2) gives d y=0; hence y belongs to E_f(C)^(i+1). Uniqueness proves additivity and R-linearity. Applying the same equation once more gives δ_(i+1)δ_i=0.

Construct the complex from these submodules with `CochainComplex.of`, not a second complex carrier. For regular f, the map x↦f^i x identifies this normalized model with the subcomplex of C[1/f] whose degree-i elements lie in f^i C^i and have differential in f^(i+1) C^(i+1). In negative degrees these powers belong in the localization. The normalized model has trivialized the line factor; it is not the literal unlocalized submodule f^i C^i.

A chain map u:C→D restricts to these submodules: fy=dx implies f u(y)=d u(x). Its divided-differential square follows by multiplying by f and using injectivity in the target. Its components are exactly the restrictions of u_i, so identities and composition follow componentwise.

**Declaration order.** `principal-term`, `principal-differential`, its specification and square-zero lemmas, `principal-complex`, `principal-map`, and its component lemma. This exposes the nontrivial division and square-zero arguments rather than hiding them inside the constructor.

### 2. Cohomology, including the precise torsion quotient

A cycle of E_f(C) is exactly a cycle of C: δx=0 implies dx=0; conversely dx=0 and injectivity imply δx=0. Under this identification, a cycle z is a boundary in E_f(C) precisely when fz is a boundary in C. Indeed, if z=δy then fz=dy; conversely dy=fz makes y an allowed term and uniqueness gives δy=z.

It follows that

\[
 H^i(E_f(C))\simeq H^i(C)/H^i(C)[f].
\]

The notation [f] means the submodule killed by f, not all elements killed by some power of f. For example, a module Z/p² concentrated in degree zero loses only its subgroup killed by p, leaving Z/p. On the free complex [R --f²→ R], the normalized divided differential is multiplication by f. This calculation is valid in degrees −3,−2 as well as 0,1. The complex [R --f→ R] instead becomes acyclic.

The three proof declarations are `principal-cycles`, `principal-boundaries` and `principal-cohomology`. Their naturality with respect to the restricted chain map is required for descent to the derived category.

### 3. The invertible-ideal construction and derived descent

For an invertible ideal I in a ringed topos, define

\[
 (\eta_I C)^i=\{x\in C^i:dx\in I C^{i+1}\}\otimes I^{\otimes i}.
\]

Termwise I-torsion-freeness identifies I⊗C^i with I C^i, so the differential factors through the next twist. On a chart I=(f), this is the preceding construction. If f′=uf for a unit u, normalized degree-i coordinates change by u^(−i). These maps commute with the divided differentials and satisfy the transition cocycle; the invertible-ideal object is consequently independent of the chosen generator.

The intrinsic cohomology statement is

\[
 H^i(\eta_I C)\simeq
 (H^i(C)/H^i(C)[I])\otimes I^{\otimes i},
\]

where H[I] is the kernel of H→H⊗I^(-1). Flatness of the invertible line lets the local cycle and boundary calculations glue. This proves that η_I preserves quasi-isomorphisms between termwise I-torsion-free complexes.

Use termwise-flat K-flat representatives and comparison roofs from E1 to obtain Lη_I on the chosen derived category. Termwise flatness is an explicit input: the label K-flat alone does not imply termwise I-injectivity. BMS1 Lemma 6.1 refers to Stacks 077J for the needed resolution construction; its generating sheaves and sequential colimit argument belong to the E1 supplier. Mathlib's `DerivedCategory` and `DerivedCategory.Q` supply the ordinary localization carrier, not this resolution theorem or an enhanced monoidal structure.

Lη_I commutes with filtered colimits and canonical truncations, as in BMS1 Corollary 6.5. The latter are good truncations, not brutal truncations; a boundary quotient of an I-torsion-free complex need not remain I-torsion-free. Its representative comparison is a separate proof obligation. No exact-functor instance is allowed: the short exact sequence 0→Z/p→Z/p²→Z/p→0 has zero outer images under Lη_p but nonzero middle image.

### 4. Multiplication

For homogeneous x,y of degrees i,j, the signed Leibniz rule puts x⊗y in the required divisibility submodule when dx and dy are divisible by f. Globally the tensor factor is the canonical map I^i⊗I^j→I^(i+j). This constructs the natural lax monoidal comparison

\[
 L\eta_I C\otimes^L L\eta_I D\longrightarrow
 L\eta_I(C\otimes^L D).
\]

Even when C and D are chosen K-flat, η_I C and η_I D are not thereby K-flat. Resolve the source factors before comparing their derived tensor with the ordinary tensor on which the element formula was defined. Associativity, signed symmetry and the unit must be compared before localization. The comparison is not asserted to be an equivalence in general; the valuation-ring improvement is a distinct theorem.

### 5. The Bockstein complex and reduction

For C in the derived category, define B_I(C)^i=H^i(C⊗^L O/I)⊗I^i. The coefficient sequence 0→I/I²→O/I²→O/I→0 defines its Bockstein differential, with the conormal factor absorbed into the next line twist.

On a principal chart, lift a residue cocycle to x with dx=fy and set β[x]=[y]. Changing x by fz changes y by dz; changing a cohomology representative likewise changes only a boundary. Also dy=0, so β²=0. For [R --f→ R], β is the identity between the two copies of R/f, not zero. For [R --f²→ R], it is zero. These are different tests.

The map η_I C modulo I→B_I(C) sends a normalized term x to its residue cohomology class. It respects differentials by construction. Both ordinary reductions compute the derived reductions because the bounded flat resolution [I→O] has injective scalar map on every term; this argument does not assert that C or η_I C is K-flat.

For injectivity on cohomology, write a cycle of E/fE as x with dx=f²z. If its image is a Bockstein boundary, choose y with dy=fw and write x−w=dv+fa. Replace y by y+fv to obtain x−δ(y+fv)=fa. Differentiating gives da=fz, so a belongs to E. Thus x is a boundary modulo fE.

For surjectivity, a β-closed class has a lift x with dx=fy and y=dz+fw. Replace x by x−fz. Its differential is f²w; applying d and cancelling f² gives dw=0, so the corrected lift is a cycle of E/fE. This proves the comparison with its differential, not merely an identification of graded cohomology modules. The generic chain map and the two lifting arguments are to be separately exposed in the eventual sheaf implementation.

### 6. Completion: two different statements

For BMS1 §6.2, T is replete and J is locally finitely generated. If C is derived J-complete, then Lη_I C is derived J-complete. The proof uses the cohomology criterion and closure under kernels and cokernels of maps **between complete modules**. It is not closure under every submodule of a complete module. Apply this to H^i(C)→H^i(C)⊗I^(-1), then to its torsion quotient, and tensor with I^i.

For completion at I itself, write C→Ĉ. Lemma 6.20 gives two comparisons, separated into two declarations:

\[
 \widehat{L\eta_I C}\longrightarrow L\eta_I\widehat C
 \longrightarrow R\!\varprojlim_n L\eta_I(C\otimes^L O/I^n).
\]

The first map exists by completeness preservation and the completion adjunction. After localizing to I=(f), reduction modulo f detects equivalences between derived complete objects. The Bockstein model agrees before and after completion because the finite derived coefficient reductions and their connecting maps agree.

For the second comparison, the perfect coefficient complex [I→O] permits reduction to pass through the inverse limit. For a complex D of O/f-modules, D⊗^L O/f^n is computed by the two-term resolution. The transition is identity on its unshifted summand and multiplication by f on the extra shifted summand; on D the latter is zero. The extra tower is therefore pro-zero. The enhanced inverse-limit argument and its sheaf-local detection are explicit E2/E4 inputs, not an unrestricted exchange of limits and cohomology.

### 7. A regression for unrelated completion

This example is an independent calculation motivated by the warning after BMS1 Lemma 6.19; it is not attributed to the paper as a printed example.

Put B=Q[x], M=⊕_(n≥0) B e_n, S(e_0)=0 and S(e_(n+1))=e_n. Let F=S−x. A nonzero finitely supported vector has a largest nonzero coordinate a_N. Its image has Nth coordinate −x a_N≠0, so F is injective.

Let A=B[t] act by t=F, take I=(t), and complete at J=(x). Both t and x are regular in A. Since x acts injectively on M, the free resolution [A --x^r→A] computes M⊗^L A/x^r as (M/x^rM)[0]. The quotient tower is surjective. The regular-principal completion theorem therefore identifies the derived completion with N[0], where

\[
 N=\{(a_n)\in\prod_{n\geq0}\mathbf Q[[x]]:
       \text{for every }r,\ a_n\in x^r\mathbf Q[[x]]
       \text{ for all but finitely many }n\}.
\]

This is a restricted sequence module, not the full product: a compatible finite-level vector has finite support at each precision, which is exactly the displayed condition. Conversely that condition constructs compatible finite-support vectors modulo x^r. The extended action is F(a)_n=a_(n+1)−xa_n.

The equation F(a)=0 gives a_n=x^n a_0, and every such sequence satisfies the restriction condition. Hence

\[
 N[t]=\{(a,xa,x^2a,\ldots):a\in\mathbf Q[[x]]\}\ne0.
\]

The finite vectors v_N=Σ_(0≤n<N)x^n e_n satisfy F(v_N)=−x^N e_(N−1); they converge to the kernel element (1,x,x²,…) in N.

Because t is injective on M, Lη_t M[0]=M[0], whereas Lη_t N[0]=(N/N[t])[0]. Naturality of the cohomology quotient and the completion adjunction identify the canonical comparison

\[
 \operatorname{completion}_x(L\eta_t M[0])
 \longrightarrow L\eta_t(\operatorname{completion}_x M[0])
\]

with the quotient N→N/N[t]. Its nonzero kernel proves that it is not a quasi-isomorphism.

The conclusion concerns this **canonical comparison**. It is not a claim that N and N/N[t] are abstractly nonisomorphic: F is surjective on N. Given b∈N, choose a_0=0 and a_(n+1)=xa_n+b_n; for each r the finitely many early summands become divisible by x^r and the later b_n already are, so a∈N. Thus F itself identifies N/N[t] with N, but it does not turn the canonical quotient into an isomorphism.

## AI.4. The specialization maps

The tilde-theta specialization yields the Hodge–Tate cohomology sheaves with their line twists. Its Bockstein recovers the de Rham differential. The theta specialization gives the de Rham complex; these two scalar maps are not interchangeable.

Use CR.4's de Rham–Witt construction and CR.0's PD envelopes for the local and A_cris comparisons. Prove independence of coordinates and presentation. The W(k) comparison is a derived base change, with its Tor terms retained. For the étale comparison invert mu, not merely p, and use the generic fibre's cohomology with the necessary derived p-completion.

**Source route and tests.** BMS1 §§8,10–12 and Theorem 1.10; torus calculation, Bockstein differential, both theta maps and a Tor-dependent specialization. These are geometric comparison proofs in addition to the generic AI.1 reduction theorem.

## AI.5. Proper cohomology and torsion

For smooth proper formal X/O_C set RΓ_Ainf(X)=RΓ(X,AΩ). Prove perfectness and finite presentation of cohomology, then the BKF conditions. Globalize the theta, W(k), A_cris and mu-inverted comparisons as maps of derived objects, preserving products, Frobenius and functoriality.

AI.5 supplies the generic specialization algebra used by CP.5: when passing from derived tensor to degreewise cohomology, adjacent-degree Tor terms must be displayed. Torsion hypotheses in a lattice-recovery statement are hypotheses in both relevant degrees, not consequences of the total complex being perfect. A torsion-length inequality is not an assertion of a subquotient relation between integral crystalline and étale groups.

**Source route and tests.** BMS1 §§4 and 14, Theorems 1.1 and 1.8, and the torsion examples of §2. CP.5 imports this algebra and applies it geometrically; it does not provide it as an assumed theorem field.
