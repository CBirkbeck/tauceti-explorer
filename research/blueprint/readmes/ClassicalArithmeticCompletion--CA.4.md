# Classical arithmetic: CA.4 follow-up — Markoff surfaces, negative Pell radicands and the triple product

This document plans the part of layer CA.4 (classical Diophantine equations) of *Classical arithmetic, sequences, polynomials and reciprocity* that its first pass left open. The first pass plans Pell and generalised Pell equations, sums of two, three and four squares, Fermat's descent, the Ramanujan–Nagell equation, numerical semigroups, Egyptian fractions and the coefficient-three Markoff surface with Markoff's tree; this part builds on those nodes and cites them by id. It adds the integral arithmetic of the Markoff level surfaces after Ghosh–Sarnak, the Markoff–Hurwitz varieties after Gamburd–Magee–Ronan, Chen's trace-coordinate normalisation, the negative Pell radicands of Koymans–Pagano, and the Jacobi triple product with the identities that Hirschhorn's four-square proof uses.

## Conventions

- Points of R³ are functions Fin 3 → R; the source's coordinates x₁, x₂, x₃ are the values at 0, 1, 2. M(x) = x₁² + x₂² + x₃² − x₁x₂x₃ (coefficient one) and V_k(R) = {M = k}; the coefficient-three surface of the first pass is X₃ : y₁² + y₂² + y₃² = 3y₁y₂y₃.
- Γ = Γ_{3,1}(ℤ) is the group of permutations of ℤ³ generated by the Vieta moves V_i(x) = x with x_i replaced by x_jx_l − x_i, the coordinate permutations and the changes of sign of two coordinates; narrow equivalence uses the permutations and double sign changes only. Orbits are taken in Mathlib's MulAction.orbitRel.Quotient.
- 'Exceptional' has two meanings in the sources and both are kept: an exceptional level (Ghosh–Sarnak) has an integral point with a coordinate of absolute value at most 2; an exceptional tuple (Gamburd–Magee–Ronan) lies in the orbit of one of two linear families. For n = 3, a = 1 the second notion covers only the coordinate-two lines of the levels 4 + w².
- Power series carry Mathlib's coefficientwise topology (PowerSeries.WithPiTopology); infinite products are HasProd statements, as in Mathlib's pentagonal number theorem.
- Sources are cited by section, statement number and page of the version read; arXiv versions are the final ones (Ghosh–Sarnak v3, Gamburd–Magee–Ronan v3, Chen v2, Koymans–Pagano v1).

## Markoff–Hurwitz varieties, moves and descent

The n-variable equation x₁² + ⋯ + x_n² = a·x₁⋯x_n + k of Gamburd–Magee–Ronan carries the whole layer: its case n = 3, a = 1 is the Markoff level surface of Ghosh–Sarnak and its case n = 3, a = 3, k = 0 is the coefficient-three surface of the first pass. The moves replace one coordinate by the other root of the quadratic in it; with the coordinate permutations and the even sign changes they generate the group Γ_{n,a}, which preserves every level set. Gamburd–Magee–Ronan remove two linear families of exceptional tuples, pass from all integral points to positive ones with a bounded error, and show that outside a compact ball the moves behave like a tree: the move at the unique largest coordinate decreases it, every other move makes its coordinate the new maximum. This gives the infinite descent to finitely many fundamental tuples. The automorphism group of the variety, the semigroup counts and Baragar's exponent belong to the ArithmeticDynamics Part II, which imports these carriers.

### `markoff-hurwitz-variety` — Markoff–Hurwitz varieties V_{n,a,k} and their positive points ★

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-variety` · definition · planet: Markoff–Hurwitz varieties.

Let n ≥ 1, let R be a commutative ring and let a, k ∈ R. The Markoff–Hurwitz set is V_{n,a,k}(R) = {x : Fin n → R : x₁² + ⋯ + x_n² = a·x₁x₂⋯x_n + k}, the points over R of the affine hypersurface (1.1) of Gamburd–Magee–Ronan; tuples are ordered (indexed by Fin n) and no positivity is imposed. Over ℤ its positive part is V_{n,a,k}(ℤ₊) = {x ∈ V_{n,a,k}(ℤ) : x_i > 0 for every i}. Two specialisations are the carriers of this layer: n = 3, a = 1 is the coefficient-one Markoff level surface x₁² + x₂² + x₃² − x₁x₂x₃ = k of Ghosh–Sarnak (CA.4/markoff-level-surface), and n = 3, a = 3, k = 0 is the coefficient-three Markoff surface X(R) of CA.4/markoff-triples.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- n ≥ 1; the results of Gamburd–Magee–Ronan take n ≥ 3, a ≥ 1 and k ∈ ℤ.
- R is a commutative ring and a, k ∈ R; no field, characteristic or sign hypothesis.
- The product is over all n coordinates; for n = 3 it is x₁x₂x₃.

Construction or proof:

1. Define V_{n,a,k}(R) as the zero set of F(x) = Σ_i x_i² − a·∏_i x_i − k on Fin n → R.
2. Functoriality: for a ring homomorphism f : R → S, F commutes with f coordinatewise, so f ∘ x lies in V_{n,f(a),f(k)}(S) for x ∈ V_{n,a,k}(R).
3. Symmetry: Σ x_i² and ∏ x_i are symmetric, so x ∘ σ ∈ V iff x ∈ V for every σ ∈ S_n; changing the signs of an even number of coordinates fixes both, so it preserves V.
4. Specialisations: for n = 3 the product over Fin 3 is x₁x₂x₃, so V_{3,1,k}(R) is the Markoff level set and V_{3,3,0}(R) is the coefficient-three set of CA.4/markoff-triples, by unfolding.

### `markoff-hurwitz-moves` — Markoff–Hurwitz moves and the group they generate with permutations and even sign changes

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-moves` · construction.

Let R be a commutative ring, n ≥ 1 and a ∈ R. For j ∈ Fin n the move m_j : Rⁿ → Rⁿ replaces x_j by a·∏_{i≠j} x_i − x_j and fixes every other coordinate. Each m_j is an involution of Rⁿ and maps V_{n,a,k}(R) to itself for every k ∈ R, because x_j and a·∏_{i≠j}x_i − x_j are the two roots of T² − (a∏_{i≠j}x_i)·T + (Σ_{i≠j}x_i² − k). The Markoff–Hurwitz group Γ_{n,a}(R) is the subgroup of Equiv.Perm (Fin n → R) generated by the moves m_j, the coordinate permutations x ↦ x ∘ σ⁻¹ (σ ∈ S_n) and the even sign changes x ↦ (ε_i x_i)_i (ε_i = ±1, ∏ε_i = 1); its narrow subgroup is the subgroup generated by the permutations and even sign changes alone. Every element of Γ_{n,a}(R) maps V_{n,a,k}(R) to itself. For n = 3 and a = 1 the moves are Ghosh–Sarnak's Vieta involutions V_j and Γ_{3,1}(ℤ) is their group Γ; for n = 3 and a = 3 the moves are the coefficient-three moves R_j of CA.4/markoff-vieta-involution.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- R commutative, a ∈ R; the moves are defined on all of Rⁿ.
- Γ_{n,a}(R) is the group generated by the listed maps. The source's Aut(V) is the group of all polynomial automorphisms; that it equals the generated group (Horowitz for n = 3, Hu–Tan–Zhang for n ≥ 4) is not used: only the invariance of V(ℤ) under the generated group is.

Construction or proof:

1. Define m_j x = Function.update x j (a·∏_{i≠j} x_i − x_j); the product over i ≠ j does not involve x_j, so m_j(m_j x) = x and m_j is an involution (Function.Involutive.toPerm).
2. Preservation: Σ_i x_i² − a∏_i x_i is, as a polynomial in x_j, T² − (a∏_{i≠j}x_i)T + Σ_{i≠j}x_i²; the two roots have the same value, so m_j preserves every level set.
3. Permutations and even sign changes preserve V by the symmetry API of CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-variety; a subgroup generated by maps preserving a set preserves it (Subgroup.closure induction).
4. Specialisation: for n = 3 the product over i ≠ j is the product of the other two coordinates, giving V_j(x) = (x₂x₃ − x₁, x₂, x₃) and its cyclic analogues at a = 1, and R_j at a = 3.
5. Functoriality: a ring homomorphism f commutes with each generator, f ∘ m_j(x) = m_j(f ∘ x) (with parameter f(a)).

### `markoff-hurwitz-exceptional` — Exceptional Markoff–Hurwitz tuples (Gamburd–Magee–Ronan, Definition 15)

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-exceptional` · definition.

Let n ≥ 3, a ≥ 1 and k be integers. A point x ∈ V_{n,a,k}(ℤ₊) is fundamental exceptional if, after sorting its coordinates increasingly, either (A) a = 1, x₁ = ⋯ = x_{n−3} = 1 and x_{n−2} = 2, or (B) a = 2 and x₁ = ⋯ = x_{n−2} = 1. In case (A) such an x lies in V iff (x_{n−1} − x_n)² = k − n − 1, and in case (B) iff (x_{n−1} − x_n)² = k − n + 2. The exceptional set E ⊆ V_{n,a,k}(ℤ) is the union of the Γ_{n,a}(ℤ)-orbits of the fundamental exceptional points; the other points are unexceptional. E is Γ_{n,a}(ℤ)-invariant; E ≠ ∅ only if a = 1 and k − n − 1 is a square, or a = 2 and k − n + 2 is a square; and when E ≠ ∅ the number of fundamental exceptional points with largest coordinate ≤ R is cR + O(1) for some c > 0.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- n ≥ 3, a ≥ 1, k ∈ ℤ.
- E is defined with the group generated by moves, permutations and even sign changes (CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-moves); the source uses Aut(V), which contains it.
- This notion differs from Ghosh–Sarnak's exceptional levels (a point with a coordinate of absolute value ≤ 2, CA.4/markoff-level-classification): for n = 3, a = 1 only the coordinate-two lines (2, t, t + w) of the level 4 + w² are Gamburd–Magee–Ronan exceptional.

Construction or proof:

1. Membership in case (A): Σx_i² = (n − 3) + 4 + x_{n−1}² + x_n² and a∏x_i = 2x_{n−1}x_n, so the equation is (x_{n−1} − x_n)² = k − n − 1; case (B) is the same computation with n − 2 ones.
2. Invariance of E is immediate from its definition as a union of orbits.
3. If (x_{n−1} − x_n)² = w² is fixed, the fundamental exceptional points are (1, …, 1, 2, m, m + w) (resp. (1, …, 1, m, m + w)) for m ≥ 2 (resp. m ≥ 1): one for each value of the largest coordinate, which gives the linear count.

### `markoff-hurwitz-signed-to-positive` — From V(ℤ) to V(ℤ₊): sign changes and the bounded error (Gamburd–Magee–Ronan, §2.1)

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-signed-to-positive` · theorem.

Let n ≥ 3, a ≥ 1, k ∈ ℤ and V = V_{n,a,k}. (a) The set X₀ of points of V(ℤ) ∖ E with a zero coordinate is empty if k < 0 and finite if k ≥ 0 (every coordinate satisfies x_i² ≤ k). (b) The group N of even sign changes acts freely on X = V(ℤ) ∖ (E ∪ X₀), and each N-orbit in X contains either exactly one point with all coordinates positive or exactly one point with x₁ < 0 < x₂, …, x_n; the orbits of the second kind are finite in number (none if k ≤ 0). (c) Consequently, for every R ≥ 0, |(V(ℤ) ∖ E) ∩ B(R)| = 2^{n−1}·|(V(ℤ₊) ∖ E) ∩ B(R)| + O_{n,a,k}(1), with B(R) the ℓ^∞ ball of radius R, and the error term bounded independently of R by |X₀| + 2^{n−1}·(number of orbits of the second kind).

Hypotheses and conventions:

- n ≥ 3, a ≥ 1, k ∈ ℤ; E is the exceptional set of CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-exceptional; the ℓ^∞ ball is N-invariant.

Proof sketch:

1. (a) A zero coordinate kills the product, so Σx_i² = k.
2. (b) A point with no zero coordinate is fixed by no nontrivial sign change, so N (of order 2^{n−1}) acts freely; even sign changes preserve the parity of the number of negative coordinates, and within a parity class they act transitively on sign patterns, so each orbit contains the all-positive pattern or the pattern with only x₁ negative, exactly once.
3. The second kind: x̃ = (−x₁, x₂, …, x_n) has positive coordinates and Σx̃_i² + a∏x̃_i = k, which has no solution for k ≤ 0 and finitely many otherwise (each x̃_i² ≤ k).
4. (c) E and X₀ are N-invariant; count X by orbits (2^{n−1} points each, all with the same ℓ^∞ norm) and add X₀.

Acceptance:

- n = 3, a = 3, k = 0: X₀ = {0}, there is no orbit of the second kind, and |V(ℤ) ∩ B(R)| = 4·|M ∩ B(R)| + 1 (the four sign patterns with product +1 of CA.4/markoff-tree).

### `markoff-hurwitz-moves-outside-compact` — Behaviour of the moves outside a compact set (Gamburd–Magee–Ronan, Proposition 16)

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-moves-outside-compact` · theorem.

Let n ≥ 3, a ≥ 1 and k ∈ ℤ. There is R₀ = R₀(n, a, k) such that for every R ≥ R₀, with K₀ = {x : ‖x‖_∞ ≤ R}, every unexceptional x ∈ V_{n,a,k}(ℤ₊) ∖ K₀ satisfies: (1) if x_j is a largest coordinate, every coordinate of m_j(x) is < x_j; (2) the largest coordinate of x is attained exactly once; (3) if x_j is not the largest coordinate, then m_j(x)_j is strictly larger than every other coordinate of m_j(x); (4) if x_j is not the largest coordinate, m_j(x) has at least as many distinct coordinates as x; (5) every move m_j maps x into V_{n,a,k}(ℤ₊). Part (3) holds only outside K₀ (the source's remark that it holds on all of V(ℤ₊) is false, PAPER-GAMBURD-MAGEE-RONAN-19/E3).

Hypotheses and conventions:

- Unexceptional positive tuples; K₀ may be enlarged at will.

Proof sketch:

1. Growth: if x_{n−1} < (x_n/(2a))^{1/(n−1)} for a sorted x, then a∏_{i<n}x_i < x_n/2 and x_n² ≤ a∏x_i + k gives x_n² < 2k; so outside a large ball x_{n−1} ≥ (x_n/(2a))^{1/(n−1)} and x_{n−1}² > |k|.
2. (1) (Cassels' argument) With f(T) = T² − a x₁⋯x_{n−1}T + x₁² + ⋯ + x_{n−1}² − k, whose roots are x_n and m_n(x)_n, failure of (1) gives f(x_{n−1}) ≥ 0, hence a x₁⋯x_{n−2} + k/x_{n−1}² ≤ n, so a x₁⋯x_{n−2} ≤ n: finitely many choices of x₁, …, x_{n−2}. Then either (x_n + x_{n−1})(x_n − x_{n−1}) ≤ x₁² + ⋯ + x_{n−2}² − k (finitely many x), or x_n = x_{n−1} is a root of (2 − a x₁⋯x_{n−2})T² + x₁² + ⋯ + x_{n−2}² − k (finitely many x) unless 2 − ax₁⋯x_{n−2} = 0 and x₁² + ⋯ + x_{n−2}² = k, which are exactly the fundamental exceptional families.
3. (2) If the largest entry were repeated, the move at one of its positions would not decrease it, against (1).
4. (3) m_j(x)_j − x_n = x_n(a∏_{i≠j,n}x_i − 1) − x_j > 0 unless a = 1 and x₁ = ⋯ = x_{n−2} = 1; in that remaining case x_{n−1}² + x_n² − x_{n−1}x_n = k − n + 2 has finitely many solutions, added to K₀.
5. (4) follows from (3); (5): outside a large ball, a x₁⋯x_{n−1} − x_n > 0, since otherwise x₁² + ⋯ + x_{n−1}² ≤ k contradicts x_{n−1}² > |k|.

Acceptance:

- n = 3, a = 1, k = 14: (1, 3, 4) violates (3) (m₂ gives the coordinate 1·4 − 3 = 1), so (3) fails on V(ℤ₊) itself; (1, 3, 4) lies in the compact set.
- For n = a = 3, k = 0 one may take K₀ = {(1, 1, 1)} up to permutation: every other Markoff triple has a unique largest entry and the move there decreases it (CA.4/markoff-descent-inequality).

Source: [Alex Gamburd, Michael Magee and Ryan Ronan, *An asymptotic formula for integer points on Markoff–Hurwitz varieties*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.06267v3), §2.1, Proposition 16 and its proof, pp. 13–15 (arXiv v3); Annals, Proposition 18, pp. 767–770. ### `markoff-hurwitz-infinite-descent` — Infinite descent for Markoff–Hurwitz tuples (Gamburd–Magee–Ronan, Corollary 17)

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-infinite-descent` · theorem.

Let n ≥ 3, a ≥ 1, k ∈ ℤ and K₀ as in CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-moves-outside-compact. Every unexceptional x ∈ V_{n,a,k}(ℤ₊) is carried into K₀ by a finite sequence of moves, each at the (unique) largest coordinate and each strictly decreasing the largest coordinate; the sequence is determined by x. Hence the unexceptional positive tuples lie in the union of the orbits, under the moves, of the finitely many points of V_{n,a,k}(ℤ₊) ∩ K₀. For n = a = 3, k = 0 this is Markoff's theorem: every Markoff triple reduces to (1, 1, 1) (CA.4/markoff-root-generation).

Hypotheses and conventions:

- Unexceptional positive tuples; the descent is algorithmic because at each step the largest coordinate is unique and the move there is forced.

Proof sketch:

1. Outside K₀ the move at the largest coordinate keeps the point in V(ℤ₊) (property (5)) and decreases the maximum (property (1)); unexceptionality is preserved because E is invariant.
2. The maximum is a positive integer, so after finitely many steps the point lies in K₀.
3. Reversing the steps exhibits x in the orbit of a point of K₀; K₀ ∩ V(ℤ₊) is finite.

Acceptance:

- n = a = 3, k = 0: K₀ ∩ V(ℤ₊) can be taken to be the permutations of (1, 1, 1), (1, 1, 2), (1, 2, 5) or just {(1, 1, 1)}, recovering CA.4/markoff-root-generation.
- n = a = 4, k = 0 (Hurwitz): (3, 1, 1, 1) reduces to (1, 1, 1, 1) by the move at the coordinate 3.

Depends on: [markoff-hurwitz-moves-outside-compact](#CA-4-markoff-hurwitz-moves-outside-compact), [markoff-hurwitz-exceptional](#CA-4-markoff-hurwitz-exceptional), CA.4/markoff-root-generation (first pass).

Source: [Alex Gamburd, Michael Magee and Ryan Ronan, *An asymptotic formula for integer points on Markoff–Hurwitz varieties*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.06267v3), §2.1, Corollary 17 and the following paragraph, p. 15 (arXiv v3); Annals, Corollary 19, p. 770. Every unexceptional tuple is reduced algorithmically to the compact set by moves that strictly decrease the maximal entry; for n = a = 3, k = 0 this is Markoff's reduction to (1, 1, 1), and Hurwitz and Baragar describe the fundamental solutions for k = 0.

## Markoff level surfaces and Chen's twist

Ghosh–Sarnak study M(x) = x₁² + x₂² + x₃² − x₁x₂x₃ on all integral levels k; Chen uses its zero level X, the trace-coordinate model of the Markoff surface. The first pass planned the coefficient-three surface X₃ : y₁² + y₂² + y₃² = 3y₁y₂y₃, its moves and Markoff's tree. The twist ξ(y) = 3y relates the two: it sends X₃ into X over every ring, intertwines the moves, is an isomorphism once 3 is invertible, and is a bijection on integral points, but it is the zero map over 𝔽₃. Through ξ, Markoff's theorem becomes the statement that the positive points of X are the orbit of (3, 3, 3).

### `markoff-level-surface` — The Markoff level surfaces V_k and Chen's trace-coordinate surface X

### `markoff-coefficient-three-twist` — The twist ξ(y) = 3y between the coefficient-three and coefficient-one Markoff surfaces

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-coefficient-three-twist` · construction.

For a commutative ring R let X₃(R) = {y ∈ R³ : y₁² + y₂² + y₃² = 3y₁y₂y₃} be the coefficient-three set of CA.4/markoff-triples and X(R) = V₀(R). The map ξ(y) = 3y sends X₃(R) into X(R). It intertwines the moves: ξ(R_j y) = V_j(ξ y) for the coefficient-three moves R_j and the coefficient-one moves V_j, and it commutes with coordinate permutations and with every change of sign. If 3 is a unit in R, ξ is a bijection X₃(R) → X(R) with inverse x ↦ 3⁻¹x, so the two surfaces are isomorphic over ℤ[1/3]. Over ℤ, ξ is a bijection X₃(ℤ) → X(ℤ) (CA.4/markoff-coefficient-one-zero-orbits). Over 𝔽₃, ξ is the zero map, X(𝔽₃) = {0} and X₃(𝔽₃) has nine points, so ξ is not an isomorphism of schemes over ℤ.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- R is any commutative ring for the first two statements; 3 ∈ Rˣ for the inverse.

Construction or proof:

1. M(3y) = 9(y₁² + y₂² + y₃²) − 27y₁y₂y₃ = 9(y₁² + y₂² + y₃² − 3y₁y₂y₃), which vanishes on X₃(R).
2. Moves: V_j(3y)_j = 9y_{j+1}y_{j+2} − 3y_j = 3(3y_{j+1}y_{j+2} − y_j) = 3·(R_j y)_j, and the other coordinates agree.
3. If 3 is a unit, the same computation run backwards shows that 3⁻¹x ∈ X₃(R) for x ∈ X(R), and the two maps are inverse.
4. Over 𝔽₃: M(x) = x₁² + x₂² + x₃² − x₁x₂x₃ = 0 forces x = 0 (if all coordinates are nonzero the squares sum to 0 but the product is ±1; if one vanishes the other two squares sum to 0, so both vanish), while X₃(𝔽₃) = {0} ∪ {±1}³.

### `markoff-positive-zero-orbit` — Markoff's theorem in trace coordinates: the positive points of X form one orbit

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-positive-zero-orbit` · theorem.

The positive integral points of X: x² + y² + z² = xyz (all three coordinates > 0) are exactly the points obtained from (3, 3, 3) by finite words in the coefficient-one moves V₁, V₂, V₃; in particular they form a single orbit of the group generated by the moves and the coordinate permutations (Chen's Γ), and every move maps a positive point to a positive point. Together with CA.4/markoff-coefficient-one-zero-orbits, X(ℤ) is the union of the two Γ_{3,1}(ℤ)-orbits {0} and Γ_{3,1}(ℤ)·(3, 3, 3).

Hypotheses and conventions:

- Integral points; positivity of all three coordinates.
- Chen's Γ (moves and permutations) has no sign changes; the sign changes are needed only for the statement about all of X(ℤ).

Proof sketch:

1. ξ of CA.4/markoff-coefficient-three-twist maps the positive Markoff triples M of CA.4/positive-markoff-triples bijectively onto the positive points of X(ℤ): it maps positive to positive, and conversely every coordinate of an integral point of X is divisible by 3 (CA.4/markoff-coefficient-one-zero-orbits).
2. By CA.4/markoff-root-generation every y ∈ M is a word R_{i₁}⋯R_{i_k}(1, 1, 1); applying ξ and the intertwining ξ ∘ R_j = V_j ∘ ξ gives ξ(y) = V_{i₁}⋯V_{i_k}(3, 3, 3).
3. Moves preserve positivity: if x ∈ X(ℤ) is positive then V_j(x)_j = (x_{j+1}² + x_{j+2}²)/x_j > 0, the other root of the quadratic.
4. For all of X(ℤ): a nonzero integral point has no zero coordinate and an even number of negative coordinates (its coordinate product is a sum of squares), so a double sign change makes it positive.

## The Δ-function

Ghosh–Sarnak order the vertices of the Vieta graph by Δ, the common discriminant of the three binary quadratic forms cut from the Bhargava cube with entries 1, x₁, x₂, x₃. Δ is a narrow invariant (permutations and double sign changes) but changes under the moves by an explicit product, (4.1), whose bracketed factor is positive for every point when k < 0 and for points with large coordinates when k ≥ 5. The sign of the change is then that of x_jx_l(x_jx_l − 2x_i), which singles out one Δ-decreasing move at every positive vertex and none at a negative one; this is the tree structure behind the fundamental sets.

### `markoff-delta` — Ghosh–Sarnak's Δ-function from Bhargava cubes

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-delta` · construction.

For x = (x₁, x₂, x₃) in a commutative ring put Δ(x) = (1 + x₂² − x₁² − x₃²)² − 4(x₁x₃ − x₂)². Then Δ(x) = (1 + x₁ + x₂ + x₃)(1 + x₂ − x₁ − x₃)(1 + x₃ − x₁ − x₂)(1 + x₁ − x₂ − x₃), and Δ is the common discriminant B² − 4AC of the three binary quadratic forms Q_i(u, v) = (x_jx_l − x_i)u² + (1 + x_i² − x_j² − x_l²)uv + (x_jx_l − x_i)v² ({i, j, l} = {1, 2, 3}) obtained from the three slicings of the Bhargava cube with entries 1, x₁, x₂, x₃. Δ is invariant under coordinate permutations and under changing the signs of two coordinates. Over ℤ: Δ(x) ≡ 0 (mod 4) if x₁² + x₂² + x₃² is odd and Δ(x) ≡ 1 (mod 4) if it is even; and if 2 ≤ x₁ ≤ x₂ ≤ x₃ then Δ(x) < 0 if and only if x₂ ≤ x₃ ≤ x₁ + x₂ − 2.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- Polynomial identities over any commutative ring; the congruence and sign statements over ℤ.
- Only the polynomial Δ is used in the rest of the layer; the Bhargava cube is the source's motivation and enters only through the discriminant identity.

Construction or proof:

1. Expand both sides of the factorisation (a polynomial identity in ℤ[x₁, x₂, x₃]); equivalently, the first form is a difference of squares (P − 2Q)(P + 2Q) with P = 1 + x₂² − x₁² − x₃² and Q = x₁x₃ − x₂, and each factor splits further.
2. Discriminants: for Q_i the discriminant (1 + x_i² − x_j² − x_l²)² − 4(x_jx_l − x_i)² equals Δ by the symmetry of the factorised form.
3. Invariance: the factorised form is permuted by S₃ and by (x₁, x₂, x₃) ↦ (−x₁, −x₂, x₃) (which swaps the pairs of factors).
4. Mod 4: with s = x₁² + x₂² + x₃², P ≡ 1 + s (mod 2) and 4Q² ≡ 0 (mod 4), so Δ ≡ P² (mod 4) is 0 if s is odd and 1 if s is even.
5. Sign: for 2 ≤ x₁ ≤ x₂ ≤ x₃ the factor 1 + x₁ + x₂ + x₃ is positive while 1 + x₂ − x₁ − x₃ ≤ −1 and 1 + x₁ − x₂ − x₃ ≤ 1 − x₁ < 0; so Δ has the sign of 1 + x₃ − x₁ − x₂, which is negative iff x₃ ≤ x₁ + x₂ − 2 (and Δ = 0 iff x₃ = x₁ + x₂ − 1). The criterion was also checked for 2 ≤ x₁ ≤ 40, x₂ ≤ 60, x₃ ≤ 120 by computation.

### `markoff-delta-differences` — The Δ-differences of the Vieta moves on a level surface

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-delta-differences` · lemma.

Let R be a commutative ring, k ∈ R and x ∈ V_k(R), and let {i, j, l} = {1, 2, 3}. Then (1) (x_j² − 4)(x_l² − 4) = (2x_i − x_jx_l)² − 4(k − 4), and (2) Δ(V_i x) − Δ(x) = x_jx_l(x_jx_l − 2x_i)·[2(k − 5) + (x_j² − 4)(x_l² − 4)]. Over ℤ the bracket in (2) is strictly positive in each of two cases: k < 0 (for every x ∈ V_k(ℤ)), and k ≥ 5 with |x_j|, |x_l| ≥ 3. Hence, for such x, the sign of Δ(V_i x) − Δ(x) is the sign of x_jx_l(x_jx_l − 2x_i).

Hypotheses and conventions:

- x ∈ V_k(R); the identities are polynomial identities modulo M(x) − k.
- For the positivity statements R = ℤ and k is an integer.

Proof sketch:

1. (1): expand (2x_i − x_jx_l)² = 4x_i² − 4x_ix_jx_l + x_j²x_l² and replace 4x_i² − 4x_ix_jx_l by 4(k − x_j² − x_l²) using M(x) = k.
2. (2): V_i changes only x_i, to x_jx_l − x_i; write Δ in the factorised form of CA.4/markoff-delta and reduce Δ(V_i x) − Δ(x) modulo M(x) − k (a polynomial computation, checked on 20000 random integral points of their own level sets).
3. k < 0: by (1) the bracket is (2x_i − x_jx_l)² − 2k + 6 > 0.
4. k ≥ 5 and |x_j|, |x_l| ≥ 3: then (x_j² − 4)(x_l² − 4) ≥ 25 and 2(k − 5) ≥ 0.

Acceptance:

- At x = (3, 3, 3) ∈ V₀ the bracket for i = 3 is 2(−5) + 25 = 15 and x₁x₂(x₁x₂ − 2x₃) = 27, so Δ(3, 3, 6) − Δ(3, 3, 3) = 405 = 325 + 80.
- Identity (1) is equation (8.2) of the source (with d = k − 4), used for the reciprocity obstructions.

### `markoff-delta-zero-level` — The Δ-minimum on the zero level: Δ < 0 exactly at the narrow class of (3, 3, 3)

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-delta-zero-level` · theorem.

For x ∈ V₀(ℤ): Δ(x) < 0 if and only if x is narrowly equivalent to (3, 3, 3) (a permutation of it with two signs changed), and then Δ(x) = −80; Δ(0, 0, 0) = 1; and Δ(x) ≥ 0 at every other point. Consequently, on each of the two Γ-orbits of V₀(ℤ) (CA.4/markoff-coefficient-one-zero-orbits) the function Δ attains its minimum exactly on one narrow-equivalence class, the class of the orbit's root (0, 0, 0) or (3, 3, 3).

Hypotheses and conventions:

- Integral points of the zero level; narrow equivalence is the action of permutations and double sign changes.

Proof sketch:

1. A nonzero x ∈ V₀(ℤ) has an even number of negative coordinates, so it is narrowly equivalent to a positive point; Δ is a narrow invariant (CA.4/markoff-delta), so assume 1 ≤ x₁ ≤ x₂ ≤ x₃.
2. Then (x₁² − 4)(x₂² − 4) − 16 = (2x₃ − x₁x₂)² (identity (1) of CA.4/markoff-delta-differences at k = 0) forces x₁ ≥ 3.
3. If Δ(x) < 0 then x₂ ≤ x₃ ≤ x₁ + x₂ − 2 < 2x₂ (sign criterion of CA.4/markoff-delta). Solving 2x₃ = x₁x₂ ± σ with σ² = x₁²x₂² − 4(x₁² + x₂²): for x₁ ≥ 4 the + sign contradicts x₃ < 2x₂, and the − sign gives 4x₂² ≤ x₁x₂² ≤ x₁² + 2x₂² ≤ 3x₂², a contradiction.
4. For x₁ = 3 the bound gives x₃ ∈ {x₂, x₂ + 1}; x₃ = x₂ forces x₂ = 3, and x₃ = x₂ + 1 gives 9 + x₂² + (x₂ + 1)² = 3x₂(x₂ + 1), which has no integral solution.

Acceptance:

- Δ(3, 3, 6) = 325 ≥ 0 and Δ(3, 6, 15) ≥ 0, while Δ(−3, −3, 3) = −80.
- The two orbits have minima Δ = 1 (at 0) and Δ = −80 (at the narrow class of (3, 3, 3)).

## Levels, class numbers and fundamental sets

A level k is admissible when it avoids the congruence obstructions k ≡ 3 (mod 4) and k ≡ ±3 (mod 9), exceptional when some integral point has a coordinate of absolute value at most 2, and generic when it is admissible and not exceptional; a Hasse failure is an admissible level with no integral point. The class number h_M(k) counts Γ-orbits. For generic k ≥ 5 and for every k < 0 the descent and the Δ-ordering identify the orbits with the explicit finite sets F⁺_k(ℤ) and F⁻_k(ℤ) (Theorem 1.1), which makes h_M(k) computable and settles the first Hasse failures 46 and −4. At exceptional levels the fundamental set still injects but misses the orbits through small coordinates, so h_M(k) is larger; the class-number bound of the source is planned at non-exceptional levels only. The Cayley cubic k = 4 has infinitely many orbits, one for each point (2, a, a).

### `markoff-level-classification` — Admissible, exceptional and generic levels, and Hasse failures

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-level-classification` · definition.

For k ∈ ℤ: k is admissible if k ≢ 3 (mod 4) and k ≢ ±3 (mod 9); k is exceptional if some x ∈ V_k(ℤ) has a coordinate in {0, ±1, ±2}; k is generic if it is admissible and not exceptional; and k is a Hasse failure if it is admissible and V_k(ℤ) = ∅. Equivalently, k is exceptional if and only if k = u² + v², or 4(k − 1) = u² + 3v², or k − 4 = u², for some u, v ∈ ℤ (the three cases being a coordinate 0, ±1 and ±2). Every admissible k < 0 is generic, and an exceptional k is never a Hasse failure.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- Integers k; exceptionality refers to integral points only.
- This exceptional notion (a point with a small coordinate) is Ghosh–Sarnak's; it is different from Gamburd–Magee–Ronan's exceptional families (CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-exceptional), which for n = 3, a = 1 are only the coordinate-±2 case.

Construction or proof:

1. Define the four predicates as stated; admissibility is decidable from k mod 36.
2. Characterisation: x₁ = 0 gives x₂² + x₃² = k; x₁ = ±1 gives x₂² ∓ x₂x₃ + x₃² = k − 1, i.e. (2x₂ ∓ x₃)² + 3x₃² = 4(k − 1); x₁ = ±2 gives (x₂ ∓ x₃)² = k − 4; conversely each solution gives a point, using the permutation symmetry for the other coordinates.
3. For k < 0 none of the three equations is solvable (the left sides are ≥ 0 while k < 0, 4(k − 1) < 0 and k − 4 < 0), so no point has a small coordinate.
4. An exceptional k has an integral point, so it is not a Hasse failure.

### `markoff-class-number` — Markoff class numbers h_M(k) and the fundamental sets F^±_k ★

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-class-number` · definition · planet: Markoff class numbers.

Let Γ = Γ_{3,1}(ℤ) act on ℤ³ (CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-moves). For k ∈ ℤ the orbit set of V_k(ℤ) is the set of Γ-orbits meeting V_k(ℤ) (each such orbit lies in V_k(ℤ)); h_M(k) is its cardinality, finite for k ≠ 4 (CA.4/markoff-class-number-finite) and zero exactly when V_k(ℤ) = ∅. The fundamental sets are F⁺_k(ℤ) = {u ∈ ℤ³ : 3 ≤ u₁ ≤ u₂ ≤ u₃, u₁² + u₂² + u₃² + u₁u₂u₃ = k} and F⁻_k(ℤ) = {u ∈ ℤ³ : 3 ≤ u₁ ≤ u₂ ≤ u₃ ≤ u₁u₂/2, u₁² + u₂² + u₃² − u₁u₂u₃ = k}, with h⁺(k) = |F⁺_k(ℤ)| and h⁻(k) = |F⁻_k(ℤ)|; both sets are finite. Two points are narrowly equivalent if one is obtained from the other by a permutation and a change of sign of two coordinates. The points (−u₁, u₂, u₃) with u ∈ F⁺_k(ℤ) form the set S⁺(k) of the descent, and S⁻(k) = F⁻_k(ℤ).

Hypotheses and conventions:

- k ∈ ℤ; the class number is a natural number by the convention Nat.card, so for k = 4 (infinitely many orbits) statements use the orbit set itself, not h_M(4).
- F⁺_k is relevant for k ≥ 5 and F⁻_k for k < 0; F⁺_k(ℤ) = ∅ for k < 54, since u₁² + u₂² + u₃² + u₁u₂u₃ ≥ 54 when every u_j ≥ 3.

Construction or proof:

1. Define the orbit set as the image of V_k(ℤ) in MulAction.orbitRel.Quotient Γ (Fin 3 → ℤ), and h_M(k) as its Nat.card.
2. Each orbit meeting V_k(ℤ) lies in V_k(ℤ), because Γ preserves V_k(ℤ) (CA.4/markoff-hurwitz-moves).
3. Finiteness of F⁺_k: 3u₁² + u₁³ ≤ k bounds u₁, then u₁² + 2u₂² + u₁u₂² ≤ k bounds u₂, and u₃ is a root of a quadratic. Finiteness of F⁻_k: u₃ ≤ u₁u₂/2 makes u₃ the smaller root of u₃² − u₁u₂u₃ + u₁² + u₂² − k, and u₃ ≥ u₂ then gives (u₁ − 2)u₂² ≤ u₁² − k, which bounds u₂ (and u₁ ≤ u₂) in terms of |k|; this is the box of CA.4/markoff-descent-negative-level.
4. h_M(k) = 0 iff V_k(ℤ) = ∅, since the orbit set is empty exactly then.

### `markoff-descent-positive-level` — Descent on V_k(ℤ) for k ≥ 5 (Ghosh–Sarnak, Lemma 2.1)

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-descent-positive-level` · theorem.

(a) Let k ≥ 1 be an integer. If 3 ≤ x₁ ≤ x₂ ≤ x₃ and x ∈ V_k(ℤ), then x₁x₂ − x₃ < x₂. (b) Let k ≥ 5. Every point of V_k(ℤ) is Γ-equivalent either to a point having a coordinate in {0, ±1, ±2} or to a point (−u₁, u₂, u₃) with u ∈ F⁺_k(ℤ). In particular, for k ≥ 5 not exceptional, every point of V_k(ℤ) is Γ-equivalent to a point of S⁺(k).

Hypotheses and conventions:

- k ≥ 1 for (a) (the source's estimate needs only k > 0 and x₁ ≥ 3); k ≥ 5 for (b) (the source states k > 4).
- The descent applies the move V₃ to a narrowly reduced positive point; small-coordinate points are kept as a separate branch (including coordinate ±2), not assumed to lie in S⁺(k).

Proof sketch:

1. Narrow reduction: a point with no zero coordinate is narrowly equivalent to one with all coordinates positive or with exactly one negative coordinate; sort the absolute values.
2. (a) The roots of c² − x₁x₂c + x₁² + x₂² − k are x₃ and x₁x₂ − x₃, namely (x₁x₂ ± σ)/2 with σ² = x₁²x₂² − 4(x₁² + x₂² − k). 3. (b) For a sorted positive point with x₁ ≥ 3 apply V₃: the new third coordinate x₁x₂ − x₃ is negative (giving a one-negative point, which is in S⁺(k) after sorting if all absolute values are ≥ 3), or in {0, 1, 2} (a small coordinate), or ≥ 3 and < x₂ by (a), giving a positive point whose coordinate sum is smaller (x₁x₂ − x₃ < x₂ ≤ x₃).
4. The coordinate sum (Hurwitz's height x₁ + x₂ + x₃) strictly decreases along the positive branch, so the process stops; a one-negative point with all absolute values ≥ 3 is (−u₁, u₂, u₃) with u ∈ F⁺_k(ℤ) after a narrow move.

### `markoff-fundamental-sets` — Ghosh–Sarnak's theorem on fundamental sets (Theorem 1.1) ★

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-fundamental-sets` · theorem · planet: Ghosh–Sarnak fundamental sets.

(i) Let k ≥ 5 be an integer that is not exceptional. The map u ↦ Γ·(−u₁, u₂, u₃) is a bijection from F⁺_k(ℤ) onto the set of Γ-orbits of V_k(ℤ): the points (−u₁, u₂, u₃), u ∈ F⁺_k(ℤ), are pairwise Γ-inequivalent and every x ∈ V_k(ℤ) is Γ-equivalent to exactly one of them. (ii) Let k < 0. The map u ↦ Γ·u is a bijection from F⁻_k(ℤ) onto the set of Γ-orbits of V_k(ℤ). Hence h_M(k) = h⁺(k) in case (i) and h_M(k) = h⁻(k) in case (ii); in particular h_M(k) is finite and is computed by a finite search.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- (i) k ≥ 5 not exceptional (the source assumes k generic; for k not admissible both sides are empty).
- (ii) every k < 0 (the source assumes k admissible; otherwise both sides are empty).

Proof sketch:

1. Surjectivity in (i): by CA.4/markoff-descent-positive-level, since k is not exceptional no point has a small coordinate, so every point is equivalent to a point of S⁺(k).
2. Injectivity in (i): CA.4/markoff-positive-root-orbits (the Δ-ordering: a negative node has three Δ-larger neighbours, a positive node exactly one Δ-smaller neighbour, so each component of the Vieta graph modulo narrow equivalence has one negative root).
3. Surjectivity in (ii): CA.4/markoff-descent-negative-level.
4. Injectivity in (ii): by CA.4/markoff-delta-differences all brackets are positive for k < 0, so the sign of Δ(V_i x) − Δ(x) is that of x_jx_l − 2x_i; at a sorted point the first two are positive and the third is ≥ 0 exactly on S⁻(k). ### `markoff-cayley-cubic-orbits` — The Cayley cubic k = 4: one orbit for each (2, a, a)

### `markoff-class-number-upper-bound` — Upper bounds for the class numbers at non-exceptional levels (Ghosh–Sarnak, consequence (c))

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-class-number-upper-bound` · theorem.

(a) For every integer k ≥ 5, |F⁺_k(ℤ)| ≤ 2k^{2/3}; for every k < 0, |F⁻_k(ℤ)| ≤ 2(|k| + 9). Hence h_M(k) ≤ 2|k|^{2/3} for k ≥ 5 not exceptional and h_M(k) ≤ 2(|k| + 9) for k < 0. (b) For every ε > 0, h_M(k) ≪_ε |k|^{1/3 + ε} as |k| → ∞ through levels k ≥ 5 that are not exceptional and through k < 0.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- (b) is the source's bound, restricted to non-exceptional levels, which is the range its argument covers (source issue ClassicalArithmeticCompletion/E553).
- The input of (b), a bound ≪_ε N^ε for the number of unit-classes of solutions of X² − (a² − 4)Y² = 4N uniform in a, is a recorded gap.

Proof sketch:

1. (a) For u ∈ F⁺_k(ℤ): 3u₁² + u₁³ ≤ k gives u₁ ≤ k^{1/3}, and u₁u₂² ≤ u₁u₂u₃ ≤ k gives u₂ ≤ (k/u₁)^{1/2}; u₃ is the unique positive root of a quadratic. Summing (k/u₁)^{1/2} over u₁ ≤ k^{1/3} gives ≤ 2k^{2/3}. For F⁻_k use the box U(k) of CA.4/markoff-descent-negative-level, with at most two values of u₃ for each (u₁, u₂). The class-number statement is CA.4/markoff-fundamental-sets.
2. (b) Fix u₁ = a ≥ 3 and put N = k − a²; the pairs (u₂, u₃) satisfy (2u₃ + au₂)² − (a² − 4)u₂² = 4N, a norm equation in the order of discriminant a² − 4, on which the unit (a + √(a² − 4))/2 acts. The fundamental-set inequalities a ≤ u₂ ≤ u₃ (and u₃ ≤ au₂/2 for k < 0) cut out a region meeting each unit-orbit in O(1) points.
3. The number of unit-orbits of solutions is at most the number of solutions b (mod 2N) of b² ≡ a² − 4 (mod 4N), summed over the square divisors of N, which is ≪_ε N^ε by the divisor bound (the recorded gap).
4. Summing over a ≤ |k|^{1/3} gives ≪_ε |k|^{1/3+ε}.

## Polynomial parametric solutions

A Vieta move lowers the largest degree of a polynomial parametric solution, so a parametric family can be taken with one constant coordinate; the leading coefficients then force the constant to be ±2 and k − 4 to be a square, which is why generic levels carry no lines. The same argument classifies the families on the companion surface x₁² + x₂² − x₃² − x₁x₂x₃ = k.

<a id="CA-4-markoff-parametric-degree-descent"></a>

### `markoff-parametric-degree-descent` — Degree descent for polynomial parametric solutions (Ghosh–Sarnak, Lemma 5.1)

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-parametric-degree-descent` · lemma.

Let ε = ±1, α_j, β_j ∈ ℤ and M*(x) = Σ_j α_jx_j + β₁x₂x₃ + β₂x₁x₃ + β₃x₁x₂ + x₁² + x₂² + εx₃² − x₁x₂x₃, and k ∈ ℤ. If P₁, P₂, P₃ ∈ ℤ[t] all have positive degree and M*(P₁, P₂, P₃) = k identically, then there are q ∈ ℤ and Q₁, Q₂ ∈ ℤ[t] of positive degree with M*(q, Q₁, Q₂) = k, or M*(Q₁, q, Q₂) = k, or M*(Q₁, Q₂, q) = k identically (one coordinate constant). No identity M*(P₁, P₂, P₃) = k holds with exactly two of the P_j constant and the third of positive degree.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- Polynomials over ℤ; 'parametric solution' means a polynomial map t ↦ (P₁(t), P₂(t), P₃(t)) into M* = k.

Proof sketch:

1. Degrees d_j = deg P_j ≥ 1: comparing the top degree of M*(P) forces the largest degree to be the sum of the other two (equal degrees, or two equal maxima, would leave the cubic term uncancelled); say d₃ = d₁ + d₂.
2. The quadratic εx₃² + (β₁x₂ + β₂x₁ + α₃ − x₁x₂)x₃ + (rest) in x₃ has a second root x₄ with x₃ + x₄ = ε(x₁x₂ − α₃ − β₁x₂ − β₂x₁) and x₃x₄ = ε(x₁² + x₂² + β₃x₁x₂ + α₁x₁ + α₂x₂ − k); so P₄ = ε(P₁P₂ − α₃ − β₁P₂ − β₂P₁) − P₃ is a polynomial solution with d₃ + d₄ ≤ 2 max(d₁, d₂), hence d₄ < max(d₁, d₂).
3. Replacing P₃ by P₄ lowers the maximal degree; repeat (with the move in whichever coordinate has the largest degree) until some coordinate becomes constant.
4. With two constants q₁, q₂ the identity reads εP₃² + (linear in P₃) + const = k, impossible for deg P₃ ≥ 1 since ε = ±1.

Acceptance:

- For M itself (all α_j, β_j = 0, ε = 1) the curve (t(t + w) − 2, t, t + w) on V_{4+w²} has degrees (2, 1, 1); one descent step, the move in the first coordinate, returns the line (2, t, t + w).
- The second-root formula of the source has sign slips that do not affect the degree count (source issue ClassicalArithmeticCompletion/E552).

Depends on: [markoff-level-surface](#CA-4-markoff-level-surface), `mathlib:Polynomial.natDegree_mul`, `mathlib:Polynomial.natDegree_add_eq_left_of_natDegree_lt`.

Source: [Amit Ghosh and Peter Sarnak, *Integral points on Markoff type cubic surfaces*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.06712v3), §5.1, Lemma 5.1 and its proof, p. 16. For the general Markoff-type polynomial M* with ε = ±1, a polynomial parametric solution with all three coordinates nonconstant can be reduced by Vieta moves, lowering the largest degree, to one with a constant coordinate; parametric solutions with two constant coordinates do not exist.

### `markoff-parametric-families` — Polynomial parametric families on V_k and on x₁² + x₂² − x₃² − x₁x₂x₃ = k

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-parametric-families` · theorem.

(1) V_k contains a nonconstant polynomial curve t ↦ (P₁(t), P₂(t), P₃(t)) with P_j ∈ ℤ[t] if and only if k − 4 = w² is a square, in which case (2, t, t + w) is one (a line); in particular there is none when k is generic. (2) The surface W_k : x₁² + x₂² − x₃² − x₁x₂x₃ = k contains such a curve if and only if k + 4 = w² is a square (family (t + w, t, 2)) or k = 0 (family (Q, 0, ±Q) for any Q ∈ ℤ[t]); moreover, for k = 4^α k′ with k′ odd, W_k has the integral point (0, 2^α(k′ + 1)/2, 2^α(k′ − 1)/2).

Hypotheses and conventions:

- Polynomials with integer coefficients; a curve is nonconstant if some coordinate has positive degree.
- The further claims of the source about W_k (no local obstructions, infinitely many integral points, infinitely many Hasse failures such as k = 94) are not planned: the source asserts them without proof.

Proof sketch:

1. By CA.4/markoff-parametric-degree-descent (and the impossibility of two constant coordinates) a nonconstant curve can be taken of the form with one constant coordinate q and two coordinates Q₁, Q₂ of the same positive degree.
2. (1) Q₁² + Q₂² − qQ₁Q₂ = k − q²: the leading coefficients q₁, q₂ satisfy q₁² + q₂² − qq₁q₂ = 0 with q₁q₂ ≠ 0, so q² − 4 is a square and q = ±2; then (Q₁ ∓ Q₂)² = k − 4 identically, so k − 4 is a square. Conversely (2, t, t + w) works.
3. (2) With the constant in the third coordinate the computation is the same with k + 4 in place of k − 4, giving (t + w, t, 2). With the constant q in the first or second coordinate, Q₁² − Q₂² − qQ₁Q₂ = k − q² forces q₁² − q₂² − qq₁q₂ = 0 for the leading coefficients, whose discriminant q² + 4 is a square only for q = 0; then (Q₁ − Q₂)(Q₁ + Q₂) = k with Q₁, Q₂ nonconstant forces k = 0 and Q₁ = ±Q₂.
4. The point: with x₁ = 0, W_k becomes x₂² − x₃² = k, and (2^α(k′ + 1)/2)² − (2^α(k′ − 1)/2)² = 4^α·k′ = k.

Acceptance:

- k = 13 = 4 + 3²: (2, t, t + 3) ⊂ V₁₃; k = 46 (generic): no parametric family.
- W₅ = W_{9−4}: (t + 3, t, 2) ⊂ W₅.

### `markoff-explicit-local-points` — Explicit points on V_k over rings in which 2 and 3 are units (Ghosh–Sarnak, Corollary 6.3)

### `quadratic-gauss-sum-normalisation` — The quadratic exponential sum S_p(a) and its comparison with Mathlib's Gauss sums

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/quadratic-gauss-sum-normalisation` · definition.

For an odd prime p let ψ = ZMod.stdAddChar : ℤ/pℤ → ℂ (ψ(t) = e^{2πit/p}), χ the quadratic character of 𝔽_p with values in ℂ, and S_p(a) = Σ_{u ∈ 𝔽_p} ψ(a u²). Then (i) S_p(0) = p; (ii) S_p(a) = χ(a)·S_p(1) for a ≠ 0; (iii) S_p(1) = gaussSum χ ψ; (iv) S_p(1)² = χ(−1)·p; (v) Σ_{a ∈ 𝔽_p} χ(a)ψ(ab) = χ(b)·S_p(1) for every b; (vi) Σ_{x ∈ 𝔽_p} χ(c − x²) = −χ(−1) for every c ≠ 0.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- p an odd prime; ψ is the standard primitive additive character; χ is quadraticChar (ZMod p) composed with ℤ → ℂ.

Construction or proof:

1. (i) is immediate. For (ii)–(iii): Σ_u ψ(au²) = Σ_t (1 + χ(t))ψ(at) by quadraticChar_card_sqrts; Σ_t ψ(at) = 0 for a ≠ 0 (AddChar.sum_mulShift), and Σ_t χ(t)ψ(at) = χ(a)⁻¹·gaussSum χ ψ = χ(a)·gaussSum χ ψ (gaussSum_mulShift, χ(a)² = 1).
2. (iv) is gaussSum_sq for the nontrivial quadratic character χ and the primitive character ψ.
3. (v) is the same computation as (iii) with the roles of the variables exchanged, using χ(0) = 0 for b = 0.
4. (vi): Σ_x χ(c − x²) = Σ_y χ(y)·#{x : x² = c − y} = Σ_y χ(y)(1 + χ(c − y)) = Σ_y χ(y(c − y)), since Σ_y χ(y) = 0 (quadraticChar_sum_zero). For y ≠ 0, χ(y(c − y)) = χ(y²)χ(c/y − 1) = χ(c/y − 1), and y ↦ c/y − 1 is a bijection from 𝔽_p^× onto 𝔽_p ∖ {−1}; so the sum is Σ_t χ(t) − χ(−1) = −χ(−1).

### `markoff-type-point-count-mod-p` — Points of x₁² + x₂² + x₃² − αx₁x₂x₃ = β over 𝔽_p (Ghosh–Sarnak, Lemma 6.4)

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-type-point-count-mod-p` · theorem.

Let p be an odd prime, α, β ∈ 𝔽_p and N_p = #{x ∈ 𝔽_p³ : x₁² + x₂² + x₃² − αx₁x₂x₃ = β}. Then N_p = p² + p·χ(−β) if α = 0, and N_p = p² + 1 + p·χ(α²β − 4)·(3 + χ(β)) if α ≠ 0, where χ is the quadratic character (χ(0) = 0). Consequently N_p ≥ (p − 2)² − 3 > 0 for p ≥ 5, N₃ > 0 for all α, β, and |X*(𝔽_p)| = p² + 3p·χ(−1), that is p(p + 3) for p ≡ 1 (mod 4) and p(p − 3) for p ≡ 3 (mod 4).

Hypotheses and conventions:

- p odd prime; α, β ∈ 𝔽_p arbitrary.

Proof sketch:

1. Reduction: for α ≠ 0 the substitution y = αx turns the equation into y₁² + y₂² + y₃² − y₁y₂y₃ = α²β, so it suffices to treat α ∈ {0, 1}.
2. α = 1: with u = 2x₃ − x₁x₂ the count equals that of 4(x₁² + x₂²) + u² − x₁²x₂² = 4β; detect the equation by orthogonality of ψ and expand with S_p (CA.4/quadratic-gauss-sum-normalisation), giving N_p − p² = (1/p)Σ_{a≠0} T_p(a)S_p(a)ψ(−4aβ) with T_p(a) = Σ_{x₁} ψ(4ax₁²)S_p(a(4 − x₁²)).
3. Split x₁ = ±2 from the other values, use S_p(a(4 − x₁²)) = χ(a(4 − x₁²))S_p(1), sum over a with (v) of the normalisation node and evaluate Σ_x χ(4 − x²) = −χ(−1) by (vi); with S_p(1)² = χ(−1)p this gives p² + 1 + p·χ(β − 4)(3 + χ(β)).
4. α = 0: N_p − p² = (1/p)Σ_{a≠0}ψ(−βa)S_p(a)³ = (S_p(1)³/p)Σ_a χ(a)ψ(−βa) = p·χ(−β).
5. Lower bound: |χ(·)| ≤ 1 gives N_p ≥ p² − 4p + 1 when α ≠ 0 and N_p ≥ p² − p when α = 0; for p = 3 check the nine pairs (α, β).

### `markoff-local-solubility` — Local solubility of the Markoff equation (Ghosh–Sarnak, Proposition 6.1) ★

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-local-solubility` · theorem · planet: Local solubility of Markoff surfaces.

(b) V_k(ℤ₃) ≠ ∅ if and only if k ≢ ±3 (mod 9), and then M(x) ≡ k (mod 3ⁿ) is soluble for every n; if k ≡ ±3 (mod 9) there is no solution modulo 9. (c) V_k(ℤ₂) ≠ ∅ if and only if k ≢ 3 (mod 4); if k ≡ 3 (mod 4) there is no solution modulo 4. Hence V_k(ℤ_p) ≠ ∅ for every prime p if and only if k is admissible, equivalently M(x) ≡ k (mod m) is soluble for every m ≥ 1 if and only if k is admissible.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- k ∈ ℤ; solubility modulo every power of p is equivalent to V_k(ℤ_p) ≠ ∅ by compactness of ℤ_p³ (or directly, since each case below gives an exact p-adic point).

Proof sketch:

2. (b) Modulo 3 the only solution of M(x) ≡ 0 is 0 (if all coordinates are units the squares sum to 0 but the product is ±1; if one vanishes, the other two squares sum to 0). ## Hasse failures

On V_{4+d} the identity w² − 4d = (x_j² − 4)(x_l² − 4) forces every prime factor of x_j² − 4 to see d as a square; for k = 4 ± 2ν², 4 + 12ν², 4 + 20ν² with the prime factors of ν in suitable classes this is incompatible with the shape of x_j² − 4 modulo 8, 12 or 20, although k is admissible. The family 4 + 2ℓ² with ℓ prime also needs the fundamental set, which bounds an odd coordinate below ℓ. Dirichlet's theorem gives infinitely many Hasse failures of each sign; their count up to K is of order at least √K/√log K, whose analytic input is a recorded gap.

### `markoff-reciprocity-obstruction` — The quadratic-reciprocity obstruction on V_{4+d}(ℤ) (Ghosh–Sarnak (8.1)–(8.2))

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/markoff-reciprocity-obstruction` · theorem.

Let d be a nonzero integer, k = 4 + d and x ∈ V_k(ℤ). For {i, j, l} = {1, 2, 3} put w = 2x_i − x_jx_l; then w² − 4d = (x_j² − 4)(x_l² − 4). Consequently every odd prime q with q | x_j² − 4 and q ∤ d has d a nonzero square modulo q (legendreSym q d = 1). In the source's form: if n ↦ (4d/n) is a primitive real character modulo 4d and S_d = {n : (4d/n) ∈ {0, 1}}, then x_j² − 4 ∈ S_d (mod 4d) for j = 1, 2, 3; by quadratic reciprocity this is a congruence condition on x_j modulo 4d that local solubility does not impose, so strong approximation for V_k(ℤ) fails modulo 4d.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- d ≠ 0 integer, x an integral point; q odd prime not dividing d.
- The character form needs (4d/·) to be a primitive Dirichlet character modulo 4d (a Kronecker symbol); the prime-factor form needs nothing.

Proof sketch:

1. The identity is (1) of CA.4/markoff-delta-differences with k − 4 = d.
2. If q | x_j² − 4 then w² ≡ 4d (mod q); as q ∤ 2d, d ≡ (w/2)² is a nonzero square modulo q.
3. Character form: (4d/·) is multiplicative and (4d/q) = (d/q) = 1 for the odd prime factors q ∤ d of x_j² − 4, while the prime factors dividing 4d give the value 0 or are absorbed; so the character of x_j² − 4 is 0 or 1. Quadratic reciprocity (jacobiSym.quadratic_reciprocity) rewrites (d/q) through congruences on q, hence on x_j.

Acceptance:

- k = 46 (d = 42): the odd prime factors of x_j² − 4 not dividing 42 are quadratic-residue primes for 42; this is consistent with V₄₆(ℤ) = ∅.
- Every point (2, t, t + w) of V_{4+w²} has x₁² − 4 = 0, the degenerate case of the statement.

## Negative Pell radicands

Koymans–Pagano count the squarefree d for which x² − dy² = −1 is soluble among the d whose prime factors are 2 or ≡ 1 (mod 4). The containment of the first set in the second is the rational solubility criterion, which here is elementary (a sum of two squares); it is strict (d = 34). Dirichlet's theorem that every prime ≡ 1 (mod 4) is a negative-Pell radicand follows from the parity of the fundamental solution of x² − py² = 1. The comparison with units of norm −1 and with 2-parts of class groups is CA.5's.

### `negative-pell-rational-solubility` — Rational solubility of the negative Pell equation

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/negative-pell-rational-solubility` · theorem.

Let d > 0 be squarefree. The following are equivalent: (i) x² − dy² = −1 has a solution x, y ∈ ℚ; (ii) d is a sum of two squares of integers; (iii) every prime factor of d is 2 or ≡ 1 (mod 4), i.e. d ∈ 𝒟. Consequently 𝒟⁻ ⊆ 𝒟. A congruence or rational criterion is not an integral one: d = 34 has the rational solution (5/3, 1/3) but no integral solution.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- d > 0 squarefree (for d not squarefree (ii) and (iii) differ, e.g. d = 9).

Proof sketch:

1. (i) ⇒ (ii): clearing denominators, X² + Z² = dY² with Y ≠ 0; so dY² is a sum of two squares, hence (Nat.eq_sq_add_sq_iff) every prime ≡ 3 (mod 4) divides dY² to an even power, hence does not divide the squarefree d, and d is a sum of two squares.
2. (ii) ⇔ (iii): Nat.eq_sq_add_sq_iff for squarefree d.
3. (ii) ⇒ (i): if d = u² + v² with v ≠ 0 then (x, y) = (u/v, 1/v) solves x² − dy² = −1; if v = 0 then d = u² is squarefree, so d = 1 and (0, 1) works.
4. This is the elementary form of the Hasse–Minkowski argument the source cites (−1 is a norm from ℚ(√d) everywhere locally).

Acceptance:

- d = 34 = 3² + 5²: (5/3, 1/3) is a rational solution.
- d = 3, 6, 21 have no rational solution (3 | d with 3 ≡ 3 mod 4).

Depends on: [negative-pell-radicand-sets](#CA-4-negative-pell-radicand-sets), `mathlib:Nat.eq_sq_add_sq_iff`.

Source: [Peter Koymans and Carlo Pagano, *On Stevenhagen's conjecture*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.13424v1), §1, the paragraph after (1.1), p. 1 (arXiv v1). Solubility of the negative Pell equation over ℚ is necessary for solubility over ℤ, and by Hasse–Minkowski it holds exactly when every prime factor of d is ≡ 1, 2 (mod 4).

<a id="CA-4-negative-pell-prime-one-mod-four"></a>

### `negative-pell-prime-one-mod-four` — Dirichlet's theorem: x² − py² = −1 is soluble for primes p ≡ 1 (mod 4)

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/negative-pell-prime-one-mod-four` · theorem.

For every prime p ≡ 1 (mod 4) the equation x² − py² = −1 has a solution in integers, i.e. p ∈ 𝒟⁻. In particular 𝒟⁻ is infinite.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- p prime with p ≡ 1 (mod 4).

Proof sketch:

1. Let (x₁, y₁) be the fundamental solution of x² − py² = 1 (Pell.IsFundamental.exists_of_not_isSquare; p is not a square).
2. x₁ is odd: if x₁ were even then y₁ would be odd and x₁² − py₁² ≡ −p ≡ −1 ≢ 1 (mod 4). Hence y₁ is even and gcd(x₁ − 1, x₁ + 1) = 2.
3. From (x₁ − 1)(x₁ + 1) = p y₁²: either x₁ − 1 = 2ps², x₁ + 1 = 2t², or x₁ − 1 = 2s², x₁ + 1 = 2pt², with s, t > 0 and y₁ = 2st.
4. In the first case t² − ps² = 1 with 0 < t < x₁, contradicting minimality; so the second case holds and s² − pt² = −1.
5. Infinitude: there are infinitely many primes ≡ 1 (mod 4) (Dirichlet's theorem, Nat.forall_exists_prime_gt_and_eq_mod).

Acceptance:

- p = 5: 2² − 5·1² = −1; p = 13: 18² − 13·5² = −1; p = 29: 70² − 29·13² = −1.
- The hypothesis p ≡ 1 (mod 4) is needed: p = 3, 7 are not in 𝒟; and primality is needed: 34 ≡ 2 (mod 4) and 221 = 13·17 ≡ 1 (mod 4) are not in 𝒟⁻.

Depends on: [negative-pell-radicand-sets](#CA-4-negative-pell-radicand-sets), CA.4/negative-pell-classification (first pass), `mathlib:Pell.IsFundamental.exists_of_not_isSquare`, `mathlib:Nat.forall_exists_prime_gt_and_eq_mod`.

Source: [Peter Koymans and Carlo Pagano, *On Stevenhagen's conjecture*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.13424v1), §1, p. 2 (arXiv v1). Dirichlet proved that every prime p ≡ 1 (mod 4) lies in 𝒟⁻, so 𝒟⁻ is infinite.

Source: [Evan Dummit, *Math 4527 (Number Theory 2), Spring 2024, Homework 3*](https://dummit.cos.northeastern.edu/teaching_sp24_4527/4527_homework_3.pdf), Problem 4 (a)–(c). ## The Jacobi triple product in formal power series

Hirschhorn's proof of Jacobi's four-square theorem in the first pass consumes the triple product and four of its specialisations as identities of formal power series. The analytic triple product for complex parameters is owned by GeometryOfNumbersAndQuadraticArithmetic (Layer 4), which precedes this roadmap; its formal counterpart is planned here by comparing coefficients, which are Laurent polynomials in the parameter, and then specialising along ring homomorphisms in Mathlib's coefficientwise topology, the convention of its pentagonal number theorem. Pak's bijection gives a purely formal route as well. QSeriesPartitionsAndMockModularForms imports these identities instead of planning them.

<a id="CA-4-power-series-product-specialisation"></a>

### `power-series-product-specialisation` — Coefficientwise identities of infinite products specialise along ring homomorphisms

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/power-series-product-specialisation` · lemma.

Let φ : A →+* B be a ring homomorphism, (f_i) a family in A⟦X⟧ and g ∈ A⟦X⟧ such that for every m the coefficient of X^m of the finite products ∏_{i∈s} f_i is eventually (in the filter of finite sets s) equal to the coefficient of X^m of g. Then the same holds for (PowerSeries.map φ (f_i)) and PowerSeries.map φ g, and HasProd (map φ ∘ f) (map φ g) holds in B⟦X⟧ with PowerSeries.WithPiTopology for every topology on B. The same holds for sums.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- A, B commutative rings; no topology on A is needed for the hypothesis.

Proof sketch:

1. coeff_m(map φ (∏_{i∈s} f_i)) = φ(coeff_m ∏_{i∈s} f_i) (PowerSeries.coeff_map and map_prod), so eventual equality transfers.
2. In PowerSeries.WithPiTopology convergence is coefficientwise (tendsto_iff_coeff_tendsto); an eventually constant sequence converges in any topology.

Acceptance:

- Applied to Mathlib's pentagonal number theorem (coeff_prod_one_sub_X_pow_eventually_eq) it gives the identity in B⟦X⟧ for every ring B, as Mathlib already states for each R.

Depends on: `mathlib:PowerSeries.map`, `mathlib:PowerSeries.coeff_map`, `mathlib:PowerSeries.WithPiTopology.tendsto_iff_coeff_tendsto`, `mathlib:PowerSeries.coeff_prod_one_sub_X_pow_eventually_eq`.

Source: [Igor Pak, *Partition bijections, a survey*](https://www.math.ucla.edu/~pak/papers/psurvey.pdf), §2.2, generating functions, pp. 10–11. Identities of partition generating functions are identities of formal power series, proved coefficientwise; the coefficient of a given degree of an infinite product is determined by finitely many factors.

### `jacobi-triple-product` — Jacobi's triple product identity in formal power series ★

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/jacobi-triple-product` · theorem · planet: Jacobi triple product identity.

Let R be a commutative ring with a Hausdorff topology (any topology for the multipliability and the coefficient form), and give R⟦x⟧ the pi topology (PowerSeries.WithPiTopology). (a) For a ∈ Rˣ the family n ↦ (1 − x^{2n})(1 + a x^{2n−1})(1 + a⁻¹x^{2n−1}) (n ≥ 1) is multipliable, n ↦ aⁿx^{n²} (n ∈ ℤ) is summable, and ∏_{n≥1}(1 − x^{2n})(1 + a x^{2n−1})(1 + a⁻¹x^{2n−1}) = Σ_{n∈ℤ} aⁿx^{n²}. (b) For s ∈ Rˣ, in R⟦t⟧: ∏_{i≥1}(1 − tⁱ)(1 + s tⁱ)(1 + s⁻¹t^{i−1}) = Σ_{k∈ℤ} s^k t^{k(k+1)/2}. Equivalently, the coefficients of the finite products are eventually equal to those of the sums. The universal cases are R = ℤ[a, a⁻¹] (Mathlib's LaurentPolynomial ℤ, a = T 1), of which every other case is the image under a ring homomorphism.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- R commutative and a, s units of R. The coefficient form needs no topology; the HasProd form holds for every topology on R, and writing the bilateral sum as a tsum assumes R Hausdorff (as Mathlib's tprod form of the pentagonal theorem does).
- The analytic identity for complex parameters, J(z; q) := (z; q)_∞(q/z; q)_∞(q; q)_∞ = Σ_{k∈ℤ}(−1)^k q^{k(k−1)/2} z^k for 0 < |q| < 1 and z ≠ 0, is owned by GeometryOfNumbersAndQuadraticArithmetic (Layer 4, §4.3, the triple product behind its Gauss identity); this node is its formal counterpart.

Proof sketch:

1. Coefficients: the coefficient of x^m of the finite products in (a) stabilises once 2n − 1 > m, and is a Laurent polynomial c_m(a) ∈ ℤ[a, a⁻¹]; likewise in (b) once i > m.
2. Complex case: (a) is J(−zx; x²) and (b) is J(−s⁻¹; t), because (−zx; x²)_∞ = ∏_{n≥1}(1 + z x^{2n−1}), (x²/(−zx); x²)_∞ = ∏(1 + z⁻¹x^{2n−1}), (x²; x²)_∞ = ∏(1 − x^{2n}) and Σ_k(−1)^k x^{k(k−1)}(−zx)^k = Σ_k z^k x^{k²}; similarly for (b) after k ↦ −k. For z ∈ ℂ^× and |x| < 1 the product converges absolutely and its expansion Σ_m c_m(z)x^m converges (majorant with |z|, |z|⁻¹, |x|), so the analytic identity and uniqueness of power-series coefficients (HasFPowerSeriesAt.eq_formalMultilinearSeries) give, for every z ∈ ℂ^× and m > 0, c_m(z) = zⁿ + z⁻ⁿ if m = n² with n > 0 and c_m(z) = 0 if m is not a square, and the analogous coefficients in (b).
3. A Laurent polynomial over ℤ vanishing on ℂ^× is zero (multiply by a power of T and use Polynomial.eq_zero_of_infinite_isRoot), so the coefficient identities hold in ℤ[a, a⁻¹].
4. Specialise along the ring homomorphism ℤ[a, a⁻¹] → R sending a to the given unit, by CA.4/power-series-product-specialisation; multipliability and summability are Mathlib's order criteria in the pi topology.
5. A purely formal alternative avoiding analysis is Pak's bijective proof of (b) (attach a Sylvester triangle to a Young diagram and split it along a diagonal).

Acceptance:

- Coefficients of x⁰, …, x⁴ in (a): 1, a + a⁻¹, 0, 0, a² + a⁻² (computation of the finite product with n ≤ 3).
- Consistency with Mathlib's pentagonal number theorem (PowerSeries.WithPiTopology.hasProd_one_sub_X_pow): in (b) the substitution t = x³, s = −x⁻¹ is legitimate coefficientwise (s^k t^{k(k+1)/2} becomes ±x^{k(3k+1)/2}, a nonnegative exponent) and turns the product into ∏(1 − x^{3i})(1 − x^{3i−1})(1 − x^{3i−2}) = ∏(1 − xⁿ) and the sum into Σ(−1)^k x^{k(3k+1)/2}.

Depends on: `GeometryOfNumbersAndQuadraticArithmetic:GN.4`, [power-series-product-specialisation](#CA-4-power-series-product-specialisation), `mathlib:LaurentPolynomial`, `mathlib:LaurentPolynomial.isUnit_T`, `mathlib:HasFPowerSeriesAt.eq_formalMultilinearSeries`, `mathlib:Polynomial.eq_zero_of_infinite_isRoot`, `mathlib:PowerSeries.WithPiTopology.multipliable_one_add_of_tendsto_order_atTop_nhds_top`, `mathlib:PowerSeries.WithPiTopology.summable_of_tendsto_order_atTop_nhds_top`.

### `jacobi-cube-identity` — Jacobi's identity ∏(1 − xⁿ)³ = Σ (−1)ⁿ(2n + 1)x^{n(n+1)/2}

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/jacobi-cube-identity` · theorem.

In R⟦x⟧ for every commutative ring R (any topology, pi topology on R⟦x⟧): ∏_{n≥1}(1 − xⁿ)³ = Σ_{n≥0} (−1)ⁿ(2n + 1) x^{n(n+1)/2}, the product multipliable and the sum summable; equivalently (1/2)Σ_{n∈ℤ}(−1)ⁿ(2n + 1)x^{n(n+1)/2} over ℤ, where the terms n and −n − 1 coincide.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- R commutative; the identity has integer coefficients and is the image of the case R = ℤ.

Proof sketch:

1. Start from form (b) of CA.4/jacobi-triple-product in ℤ[s, s⁻¹]⟦t⟧: Σ_k s^k t^{k(k+1)/2} = ∏_{i≥1}(1 − tⁱ)(1 + stⁱ)(1 + s⁻¹t^{i−1}).
2. Put s = −w: the factor i = 1 of the last product is 1 − w⁻¹, and pairing k with −k − 1 on the left gives (1 − w⁻¹)·∏_{i≥1}(1 − tⁱ)(1 − wtⁱ)(1 − w⁻¹tⁱ) = Σ_{k≥0}(−1)^k w^{−k−1}(w^{2k+1} − 1)t^{k(k+1)/2}.
3. 1 − w⁻¹ is a non-zero-divisor in ℤ[w, w⁻¹]⟦t⟧, so ∏(1 − tⁱ)(1 − wtⁱ)(1 − w⁻¹tⁱ) = Σ_k (−1)^k w^{−k}(1 + w + ⋯ + w^{2k}) t^{k(k+1)/2}.
4. Specialise w ↦ 1 (CA.4/power-series-product-specialisation): 1 + w + ⋯ + w^{2k} ↦ 2k + 1.

### `theta-product-specialisations` — The theta-product specialisations of the triple product

`ClassicalArithmeticCompletion:CA.4/theta-product-specialisations` · theorem.

In R⟦x⟧ for every commutative ring R (any topology, pi topology): (ii) ∏_{n≥1}(1 + x^{2n−1})²(1 − x^{2n}) = Σ_{n∈ℤ} x^{n²}; (iii) 2·∏_{n≥1}(1 + x^{2n})²(1 − x^{2n}) = Σ_{r∈ℤ} x^{r²+r}, equivalently ∏_{n≥1}(1 + x^{2n})²(1 − x^{2n}) = Σ_{r≥0} x^{r(r+1)} (the form valid over every ring, since the terms r and −r − 1 coincide); (iv) (x; x)_∞ = (−x; x)_∞ · Σ_{n∈ℤ}(−1)ⁿ x^{n²}, i.e. ∏(1 − xⁿ)/(1 + xⁿ) = Σ(−1)ⁿx^{n²}, where (−x; x)_∞ = ∏(1 + xⁿ) is a unit; and Euler's identity (−x; x)_∞ · ∏_{n≥1}(1 − x^{2n−1}) = 1.

Hypotheses and conventions:

- R commutative; each identity is the image of the case R = ℤ, and the products and sums are multipliable and summable.

Proof sketch:

1. (ii): CA.4/jacobi-triple-product with a = 1.
2. (iii): form (b) of CA.4/jacobi-triple-product at s = 1, followed by the substitution t ↦ x² (coefficientwise); the factor i = 1 of the last product is 2.
3. Euler: ∏(1 + xⁿ)(1 − xⁿ) = ∏(1 − x^{2n}) and ∏(1 − xⁿ) = ∏(1 − x^{2n})∏(1 − x^{2n−1}); cancel the unit ∏(1 − x^{2n}).

## Mistakes found in the sources

- **ClassicalArithmeticCompletion/E551** (error, §2, the paragraph after Lemma 2.1, p. 10 (arXiv v3)). For k = 2 the descent leaves the point (0, 1, 1), and the source says its Γ-orbit is infinite. Correction: The Γ-orbit of (0, 1, 1) is finite: it consists of the twelve points with one coordinate 0 and the other two ±1, and the four points (±1, ±1, ±1) with an even number of minus signs, 16 points in all; it is all of V₂(ℤ), so h_M(2) = 1. Reason: V₁(0, 1, 1) = (1, 1, 1), V₂(0, 1, 1) = (0, −1, 1), V₃(0, 1, 1) = (0, 1, −1), and V_i(1, 1, 1) is (0, 1, 1) up to permutation; so the 16 points are closed under the moves, permutations and double sign changes. A search over |x_i| ≤ 40 finds no other integral point, as the descent predicts.
- **ClassicalArithmeticCompletion/E552** (misprint, §5.1, proof of Lemma 5.1, p. 16 (arXiv v3)). The second root is written x₄ = x₁x₂ − α₃ − β₁x₂ − β₂x₁ − εx₃, with P₃P₄ = k − P₁² − P₂² − β₃P₁P₂ − α₁P₁ − α₂P₂. Reason: As a quadratic in x₃, M* − k = εx₃² + (β₁x₂ + β₂x₁ + α₃ − x₁x₂)x₃ + (x₁² + x₂² + β₃x₁x₂ + α₁x₁ + α₂x₂ − k), whose roots sum to −(coefficient)/ε and multiply to (constant)/ε, with 1/ε = ε. For M itself (ε = 1, α = β = 0) the product of the roots is x₁² + x₂² − k, not k − x₁² − x₂².
- **ClassicalArithmeticCompletion/E554** (error, §1, the paragraph after Theorem 1.1 and the caption of Figures 1–2, p. 4 (arXiv v3)). Theorem 1.1, stated for generic k, is illustrated with k = 3685, for which the source gives h_M(3685) = 6. Correction: 3685 is exceptional, since (1, 32, 70) ∈ V₃₆₈₅(ℤ) (4·3684 = 108² + 3·32²), so Theorem 1.1 (i) does not apply. The six points are F⁺₃₆₈₅(ℤ), and by the corrected comparison of ClassicalArithmeticCompletion/E507, h_M(3685) ≥ h⁺_M(3685) + 1 = 7. Reason: M(1, 32, 70) = 1 + 1024 + 4900 − 2240 = 3685, a point with a coordinate 1; the orbit of a point with a small coordinate does not contain any (−u₁, u₂, u₃) with u ∈ F⁺ (CA.4/markoff-positive-root-orbits). - **ClassicalArithmeticCompletion/E553** (gap, §1, consequence (c) of Theorem 1.1, p. 4 (arXiv v3)). The source states h_M(k) ≪_ε |k|^{1/3+ε} as k → ±∞, as a consequence of Theorem 1.1. Correction: The argument, through the fundamental sets of Theorem 1.1, covers only levels that are not exceptional (generic k ≥ 5 and all k < 0). At exceptional levels the orbits through small-coordinate points are not controlled, and on the family k = 4 + w² the points (2, t, t + w) appear to lie in ⌊w/2⌋ + 1 distinct orbits, which would contradict the bound for large w.
